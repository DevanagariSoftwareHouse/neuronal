\chapter{From a physical voltage to a sampled sequence}\label{ch:sampling}
\begin{readergoal}Understand the measurement chain, units, sample rates, aliasing, and the epistemic difference between source and signal.\end{readergoal}
\section{What electrodes actually measure}
Extracellular electrodes record potential differences relative to a reference. Their outputs reflect contributions of multiple transmembrane currents, electrode geometry, and conductive tissue. The recorded LFP is not a direct readout of a particular neuron or synapse. A physical forward model connects currents to voltages \cite{Buzsaki2012,Einevoll2013}. Analog amplification, referencing, and filtering occur before the digitized values are stored.

\section{Sampled time is a lattice}
For sampling frequency $f_s$, write $\Delta t=1/f_s$ and $x[n]=x(n\Delta t)$ for integer $n$ in the acquisition index set. The Nyquist frequency is $f_s/2$. A sinusoid at a frequency above Nyquist can produce the same discrete samples as a lower-frequency sinusoid; this is \emph{aliasing}. A pre-sampling low-pass filter is therefore required to suppress frequencies that would fold into the band of interest. Merely applying a digital low-pass filter after sampling cannot undo aliasing already introduced.

\section{The ideal sampling theorem and its assumptions}
If a continuous signal is strictly bandlimited to frequencies $|f|<f_s/2$, its samples determine the original signal through ideal sinc interpolation. Actual brain recordings are neither exactly bandlimited nor free of instrumentation artifacts. The theorem establishes a mathematical possibility under stated conditions, not a promise that every sampled waveform reproduces all physical current sources.

\section{Storage, quantization, and memory mapping}
A discrete recording is represented in finite precision. With $C$ channels, $N$ samples, and $b$ bytes per stored sample, uncompressed size is $C N b$ bytes (ignoring metadata and headers). Memory-mapped storage can support access to selected time blocks without allocating the entire dataset. It does not guarantee fast noncontiguous access: array layout, strides, page faults, and disk bandwidth still matter. Always document sample type, endian convention, orientation, sampling rate, units, and channel metadata.

\begin{workedexample}[A large acquisition]
For 128 channels at 30,000 samples/s over one hour, stored as signed 16-bit integers, the raw size is $128\cdot30000\cdot3600\cdot2=27{,}648{,}000{,}000$ bytes (about 27.65 GB in decimal units). Converting the entire array to 64-bit floats multiplies memory by four. A streaming analysis therefore need not sacrifice mathematical precision in its intermediate accumulators, but should avoid careless full-array expansion.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Aliasing as an equivalence relation}
Fix a sampling rate $f_s$. Two frequencies $f$ and $f'$ produce identical samples of the complex exponential $e^{2\pi ift}$ at times $n/f_s$ iff $e^{2\pi i(f-f')n/f_s}=1$ for every integer $n$, i.e.\ iff $f-f'$ is an integer multiple of $f_s$. ``Same samples'' is thus the equivalence relation $f\sim f'\iff f-f'\in f_s\mathbb Z$ of Chapter~\ref{ch:relations}, and each class has exactly one representative in $(-f_s/2,f_s/2]$. For \emph{real} sinusoids, $\cos(2\pi ft)$ and $\cos(-2\pi ft)$ coincide, so each class of $|f|$ has a representative in $[0,f_s/2]$: the folded or \emph{alias} frequency
\[
f_{\rm alias}=\bigl|f-f_s\cdot\operatorname{round}(f/f_s)\bigr|.
\]
A 60\,Hz line-noise harmonic at 540\,Hz sampled at 1000\,Hz appears at $|540-1000|=460$\,Hz; sampled at 500\,Hz it appears at $|540-500|=40$\,Hz, inside the gamma band.

\section{Quantization}
An analog-to-digital converter with $b$ bits over an input range of width $V$ has step $q=V/2^b$. Rounding to the nearest level introduces an error $e\in[-q/2,q/2]$. If the signal varies over many levels, a standard model treats $e$ as uniform on that interval and independent of the signal, giving error variance $q^2/12$. For a full-scale sinusoid the resulting signal-to-quantization-noise ratio is approximately $6.02b+1.76$\,dB.

\begin{workedexample}[Resolution of a 16-bit headstage]
A 16-bit converter spanning $\pm5$\,mV has $q=10\,\text{mV}/65536\approx0.15\,\mu$V and quantization noise standard deviation $q/\sqrt{12}\approx0.044\,\mu$V, far below typical thermal and biological noise of several microvolts. In this regime the uniform-error model is harmless. When gain is set so low that a signal spans only a few levels, the model fails: the error becomes deterministic and signal-dependent, and it creates harmonics rather than white noise.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Resampling}
Changing the sampling rate by a rational factor $L/M$ (in lowest terms) consists of three steps: insert $L-1$ zeros between samples (upsampling), low-pass filter at the lower of the two Nyquist frequencies, and keep every $M$th sample (downsampling). Omitting the filter before downsampling folds high frequencies into the band exactly as in analog sampling: decimating a 30\,kHz wideband trace to 1\,kHz by taking every 30th sample aliases spike energy into the LFP band. Library routines that ``decimate'' include the filter; plain indexing does not.

\section{Timing and synchronization}
Two acquisition systems running at nominally equal rates drift apart because their clocks differ by a few parts per million. A 10\,ppm discrepancy accumulates to $36$\,ms per hour, larger than many spike--LFP or spike--behavior timing effects. Alignment therefore requires shared synchronization pulses, and the mapping between clocks is estimated by regression of pulse times, typically linear ($t_B=a+bt_A$) over a session. Residuals of that regression measure the timing uncertainty that should be propagated into any latency claim.

\section{Sample-and-hold and the zero-order hold}
Physical converters do not take instantaneous point samples; they integrate over a short aperture, and digital-to-analog converters hold each value until the next sample. Holding for one sample period multiplies the spectrum by $\operatorname{sinc}(f/f_s)$, an attenuation of about $3.9$\,dB at Nyquist. This is a reminder that the ``ideal sampling'' of the previous sections is a model whose deviations are small but quantifiable.
