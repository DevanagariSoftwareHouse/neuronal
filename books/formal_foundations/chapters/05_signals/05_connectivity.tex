\chapter{Cross-spectra, coherence, and Granger prediction}\label{ch:connectivity}
\begin{readergoal}Derive dependence measures for multivariate signals and understand why prediction is not intervention.\end{readergoal}
\section{From autocovariance to cross-covariance}
For weakly stationary processes $X_t$ and $Y_t$, define $C_{xy}(\tau)=E[(X_t-\mu_x)(Y_{t+\tau}-\mu_y)]$. Its Fourier transform is the cross-spectral density $S_{xy}(f)$. The autospectra $S_{xx},S_{yy}$ describe each process; the cross-spectrum is complex and contains magnitude and phase information about their lagged relationship.

Magnitude-squared coherence is
\[\mathcal C_{xy}(f)=\frac{|S_{xy}(f)|^2}{S_{xx}(f)S_{yy}(f)},\]
when the denominator is positive. For valid spectral-density matrices, $0\le\mathcal C_{xy}(f)\le1$. Coherence is non-directional. Magnitude-only summaries discard phase and cannot by themselves indicate whether one observed signal drives another.

\section{Common input and reference contamination}
Suppose $x=s+n_x$ and $y=s+n_y$ share a signal $s$ while $n_x,n_y$ are independent noise processes. Then $x$ and $y$ can exhibit substantial coherence even though no direct interaction between their unique sources exists. Similarly, a reference electrode that carries electrical activity can insert a common component into many recordings. Bipolar referencing or spatial derivatives may reduce some effects but introduce others; one must evaluate the measurement geometry and signal-to-noise ratio \cite{Bastos2016,CohenTsuchiya2018}.

\section{Autoregressive models and direction of prediction}
A vector autoregressive model of order $p$ for $z_t\in\mathbb R^d$ is
\[z_t=\sum_{k=1}^p A_kz_{t-k}+\varepsilon_t.\]
Consider predicting $Y_t$ from its own past versus predicting it from both its own past and the past of $X_t$. A classical Gaussian linear Granger statistic compares residual variances:
\[F_{X\to Y}=\log\frac{\sigma^2_{\rm restricted}}{\sigma^2_{\rm full}}.\]
When the full model predicts better, the statistic is positive. The arrow denotes an improvement in prediction under the candidate model, not proof that manipulating $X$ would change $Y$. Latent common causes, instantaneous mixing, nonstationarity, model order, and noise can alter apparent direction. Spectral Granger extensions decompose predictive relationships by frequency under additional factorization assumptions \cite{Cekic2018}.

\section{Transfer entropy and conditional dependence}
Transfer entropy is a conditional information measure, often written $I(Y_t;X_{t-1}^{(p)}\mid Y_{t-1}^{(q)})$. It compares how informative the past of $X$ is about $Y_t$ once the past of $Y$ is known. Nonlinear estimators can be useful but require much data and careful treatment of bias. Prediction-based asymmetry is not a universal causal-identification guarantee.

\begin{workedexample}[A shared driver]
Let $s_t$ be a stochastic signal, $x_t=s_t+\eta_t$, and $y_t=s_{t-1}+\xi_t$ with independent observation noises. The lag creates directional predictability from $x$ to $y$, but the actual source is $s$, not a connection from the observed $x$ channel to the observed $y$ channel. This is a constructive counterexample to an unrestricted causal interpretation.
\end{workedexample}

\section{The spectral density matrix}
For a $d$-dimensional weakly stationary process with absolutely summable cross-covariances, the \emph{spectral density matrix} $S(f)$ has entries $S_{jk}(f)$, the cross-spectra. For every $f$ it is Hermitian ($S_{kj}=\overline{S_{jk}}$) and positive semidefinite: for any $a\in\mathbb C^d$, $a^\ast S(f)a$ is the spectral density of the scalar process $\sum_j\bar a_jX_j$ and is therefore nonnegative.

\begin{proposition}
$0\le\mathcal C_{xy}(f)\le1$ whenever $S_{xx}(f)S_{yy}(f)>0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix}S_{xx}&S_{xy}\\S_{yx}&S_{yy}\end{pmatrix}$ is positive semidefinite, so its determinant $S_{xx}S_{yy}-|S_{xy}|^2$ is nonnegative.
\end{proof}

\section{Coherence under a common source}
Let $x=s+n_x$, $y=s+n_y$ with $s,n_x,n_y$ mutually uncorrelated and spectra $S_s,S_x^{n},S_y^{n}$. Then $S_{xy}=S_s$, $S_{xx}=S_s+S^n_x$, $S_{yy}=S_s+S^n_y$, and
\[
\mathcal C_{xy}(f)=\frac{S_s^2}{(S_s+S^n_x)(S_s+S^n_y)}=\frac{1}{(1+1/\mathrm{SNR}_x)(1+1/\mathrm{SNR}_y)},
\]
with $\mathrm{SNR}_x=S_s/S^n_x$. Coherence here is entirely a function of signal-to-noise ratios. A condition that increases the power of the shared signal (e.g.\ running increases theta in all electrodes) increases coherence without any change in interaction. Conversely, adding independent noise to one channel decreases coherence without any change in interaction. Comparisons of coherence across conditions are only interpretable alongside the corresponding power changes.

\section{Bias of estimated coherence}
Coherence is estimated from $K$ independent trials or tapers by $\hat{\mathcal C}=|\sum_kX_k\bar Y_k|^2/(\sum_k|X_k|^2\sum_k|Y_k|^2)$. When the true coherence is zero, $\E\hat{\mathcal C}=1/K$ for Gaussian data, so ten tapers give a baseline near $0.1$, not $0$. The same $1/K$ bias that affects the PLV affects coherence. Confidence intervals are usually constructed on the Fisher-transformed coherency $\operatorname{atanh}|\hat c|$ or by jackknife over tapers or trials.

\section{The imaginary part of coherency}
\emph{Coherency} is the complex quantity $c_{xy}=S_{xy}/\sqrt{S_{xx}S_{yy}}$. Under instantaneous linear mixing, for example volume conduction in a quasistatic medium where $x=a_1s_1+a_2s_2$ and $y=b_1s_1+b_2s_2$ with real coefficients and independent sources, the cross-spectrum is $S_{xy}=a_1b_1S_{s_1}+a_2b_2S_{s_2}$, which is \emph{real}. Hence:

\begin{proposition}
If two channels are real instantaneous mixtures of mutually uncorrelated sources, then $\Im c_{xy}(f)=0$ for every $f$.
\end{proposition}
A nonzero imaginary part therefore requires a time lag between the channels' contributions and cannot be produced by zero-lag mixing alone. This motivates the imaginary coherence and the (weighted) phase-lag index. The converse inference fails: a genuine zero-lag interaction is invisible to these measures, and lags due to differing filter delays or reference effects produce nonzero imaginary parts without interaction.

\section{Spectral Granger causality}
Write a stationary bivariate VAR in moving-average form $Z(f)=H(f)E(f)$ with innovation covariance $\Sigma$. If $\Sigma$ is diagonal, $S_{yy}(f)=|H_{yy}(f)|^2\Sigma_{yy}+|H_{yx}(f)|^2\Sigma_{xx}$, and Geweke's frequency-domain measure is
\[
f_{X\to Y}(f)=\log\frac{S_{yy}(f)}{|H_{yy}(f)|^2\Sigma_{yy}},
\]
the log ratio of total power of $Y$ at $f$ to the part of its power driven by its own innovations. (When $\Sigma$ has off-diagonal terms a normalizing transformation is applied first.) Under regularity conditions, $\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}f_{X\to Y}(f)\,df$ equals the time-domain statistic $F_{X\to Y}$. Non-parametric estimates compute $H$ and $\Sigma$ from the spectral matrix by Wilson's spectral factorization rather than fitting a VAR. All the cautions of the time-domain measure carry over, and filtering the data before estimating Granger causality can create or destroy spectral structure in it; filtering is therefore discouraged.

\begin{workedexample}[Coherence change without coupling change]
Two electrodes record $x=s+n_x$, $y=s+n_y$ with noise spectra equal to $1$ at 8\,Hz. At rest the shared theta spectrum is $S_s=1$, so $\mathcal C=1/4$. During running $S_s=4$, so $\mathcal C=(4/5)^2=0.64$. The mixing and the noise are unchanged; only the shared source power has grown. A report that ``theta coherence increased during running'' is accurate; a report that ``coupling increased'' is not supported.
\end{workedexample}
