\begin{readergoal}Understand why the real line is more than rational arithmetic, and why continuity and convergence depend on completeness.\end{readergoal}
\section{The real line as an ordered field}
We wish to represent distances, times, and voltages without rational-number gaps. The real numbers $\mathbb R$ are an \emph{ordered field} satisfying a further completeness property. One characterization is the \emph{least-upper-bound property}: every nonempty subset of $\mathbb R$ bounded above has a least upper bound. If $A=\{x\in\mathbb R:x^2<2,\ x\ge0\}$, then $\sup A=\sqrt2$. This value cannot be supplied by rational numbers alone.

A real number may be developed rigorously using Dedekind cuts or equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rational numbers; either construction requires proving that the resulting operations and order are well defined. We will use the completeness axiom without adopting one construction as primary. For a fuller foundational treatment, consult \cite{MendelsonNumbers}.

\section{Bounds, supremum, and infimum}
A number $M$ is an upper bound for $A$ if $x\le M$ for every $x\in A$. The supremum is the smallest such bound; it need not belong to the set. For the open interval $(0,1)$, the supremum is 1, although 1 is not a member. The infimum is defined dually. A \emph{maximum} must be a member of the set and greater than or equal to every other member; distinguish maximum from supremum.

\section{Distances and the triangle inequality}
The absolute value defines distance on $\mathbb R$ by $d(x,y)=|x-y|$. Distance is nonnegative, symmetric, zero precisely for equal points, and obeys $d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)$. These four properties define a \emph{metric} on a more general space. A tolerance criterion for a numerical solution is meaningful only with a specified distance, scale, and units.

\section{Interval notation and physical uncertainty}
The interval $[a,b]$ includes endpoints, whereas $(a,b)$ excludes them. An experimental measurement can be modeled as an uncertain value lying in some interval, but the interval should not be mistaken for a statistical confidence interval without an inference procedure. A confidence interval concerns the long-run coverage of an estimator under a model; it is not simply an arbitrary range surrounding a measurement.

\begin{workedexample}[The value of completeness]
Take a sequence of rational approximations $1,1.4,1.41,1.414,\ldots$ to $\sqrt2$. Each term can be rational while its limit is not. A space of numbers meant to support convergence of such sequences must contain the limiting value. The same idea underlies convergence of numerical algorithms for signal processing.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Consequences of completeness}
The least-upper-bound property is a single axiom, but nearly every existence theorem in analysis rests on it. We record the first consequences.

\begin{proposition}[Approximation property of the supremum]
Let $A\subseteq\mathbb R$ be nonempty and bounded above, and $s=\sup A$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $a\in A$ with $s-\varepsilon<a\le s$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
If no such $a$ existed, then $a\le s-\varepsilon$ for every $a\in A$, so $s-\varepsilon$ would be an upper bound smaller than the least upper bound $s$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Archimedean property of $\mathbb R$]
For every real $x$ there is a natural number $n>x$. Equivalently, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $n$ with $1/n<\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If not, $\mathbb N_+$ is bounded above by $x$, so it has a supremum $s$. By the approximation property with $\varepsilon=1$ there is $n$ with $s-1<n$, whence $n+1>s$, contradicting that $s$ bounds $\mathbb N_+$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Density of the rationals]
Between any two reals $x<y$ there is a rational number.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By the Archimedean property choose $n$ with $1/n<y-x$. Let $m$ be the smallest integer with $m>nx$ (it exists by well ordering, applied after shifting). Then $m-1\le nx$, so $m\le nx+1<ny$. Hence $x<m/n<y$.
\end{proof}
Density justifies approximating any real parameter by a finite-precision number to arbitrary accuracy. It does \emph{not} imply that every real is rational, and indeed the irrationals are also dense: if $x<y$, there is a rational $q$ with $x/\sqrt2<q<y/\sqrt2$ (for $q\ne0$), and then $q\sqrt2$ is irrational and lies between $x$ and $y$.

\section{Existence of square roots}
\begin{theorem}
There is a unique positive real $s$ with $s^2=2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $A=\{x\ge0:x^2<2\}$, which is nonempty ($1\in A$) and bounded above by $2$. Let $s=\sup A$. Suppose $s^2<2$. For $0<h<1$, $(s+h)^2\le s^2+h(2s+1)$, which is less than $2$ once $h<(2-s^2)/(2s+1)$; then $s+h\in A$, contradicting that $s$ is an upper bound. Suppose $s^2>2$. For $0<h<s$, $(s-h)^2\ge s^2-2sh>2$ once $h<(s^2-2)/(2s)$; then $s-h$ is an upper bound of $A$ smaller than $s$, a contradiction. Hence $s^2=2$. Uniqueness: if $s,t>0$ and $s^2=t^2$ then $(s-t)(s+t)=0$ with $s+t>0$, so $s=t$.
\end{proof}
The argument is a template: guess the object as a supremum, then rule out the two strict inequalities using small perturbations. The same template proves the intermediate value theorem in Chapter~\ref{ch:calculus}.

\section{Nested intervals and uncountability}
\begin{theorem}[Nested interval property]
If $[a_1,b_1]\supseteq[a_2,b_2]\supseteq\cdots$ is a decreasing sequence of nonempty closed bounded intervals, then $\bigcap_n[a_n,b_n]\ne\varnothing$. If moreover $b_n-a_n\to0$, the intersection is a single point.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The left endpoints are nondecreasing and bounded above by every $b_m$, so $a=\sup_na_n$ exists and $a_n\le a\le b_m$ for all $n,m$. Thus $a$ belongs to every interval. If two points $a<a'$ belonged to all intervals then $b_n-a_n\ge a'-a>0$ for every $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Cantor]
The interval $[0,1]$ is uncountable.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $x_1,x_2,\ldots$ be any sequence in $[0,1]$. Divide $[0,1]$ into three closed thirds; at least one does not contain $x_1$; call it $I_1$. Divide $I_1$ into thirds and pick one, $I_2$, not containing $x_2$. Continuing, we obtain nested closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$ with $x_n\notin I_n$. Their intersection contains a point $x$ that differs from every $x_n$. Thus no sequence lists all of $[0,1]$.
\end{proof}

\section{Floating-point numbers are not real numbers}
A double-precision floating-point number is a rational of the form $\pm m\cdot2^{e}$ with a 53-bit integer significand $m$ and a bounded exponent $e$. The set of such numbers is finite. It is not closed under addition (results are rounded), addition is not associative, and the distance between consecutive representable numbers grows with magnitude. The \emph{machine epsilon} $\epsilon_{\rm mach}=2^{-52}\approx2.2\times10^{-16}$ is the gap between $1$ and the next larger double. Every real $x$ in the normal range is rounded to a double $\mathrm{fl}(x)$ with relative error $|\mathrm{fl}(x)-x|/|x|\le\epsilon_{\rm mach}/2$.

\begin{workedexample}[Timestamps in long recordings]
A 24-hour recording at 30\,kHz has about $2.6\times10^9$ samples. Storing timestamps in seconds as doubles gives an absolute resolution near $t=86400$\,s of about $86400\cdot2.2\times10^{-16}\approx1.9\times10^{-11}$\,s, ample for spike timing. Storing them as single-precision floats ($\epsilon\approx1.2\times10^{-7}$) gives about $10$\,ms resolution near the end of the day, which would destroy millisecond spike timing. Storing sample indices as 64-bit integers avoids the issue entirely. Completeness of $\mathbb R$ is a property of the mathematical model, not of the stored numbers.
\end{workedexample}
