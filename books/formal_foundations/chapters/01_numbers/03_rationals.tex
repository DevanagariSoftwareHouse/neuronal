\chapter{Rational numbers, ratios, and fields}\label{ch:rationals}
\begin{readergoal}Construct fractions and learn why units, denominators, and field axioms matter in quantitative science.\end{readergoal}
\section{Fractions as equivalence classes}
Take integer pairs $(a,b)$ with $b\ne0$. Say $(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ when $ad=bc$. The resulting equivalence class represents the fraction $a/b$. Ratios like $1/2=2/4$ are different representations of the same rational number. Addition and multiplication take their familiar forms
\[\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad \frac ab\cdot\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}.\]
Rationals form the field $\mathbb Q$: nonzero values possess multiplicative inverses, and the field operations obey associativity, commutativity, and distributivity. Division by zero is undefined: a rate with zero exposure duration is not an extreme number but an invalid operation.

\section{What does a ratio mean physically?}
Suppose a unit emits 75 spikes over 15 seconds. Its time-averaged rate is $75/15=5$ spikes/s. The numerator and denominator have different roles and units. When a software routine returns a mean across observations, ask what each observation represents and whether every observation has the same weight. The mean of within-trial rates need not equal total count divided by total exposure if trial durations differ.

\section{Rationals are dense but incomplete}
Between any two rational numbers $a<b$, their midpoint $(a+b)/2$ is another rational. Yet the equation $x^2=2$ has no rational solution. To show this, suppose $x=p/q$ is in lowest terms and $p^2=2q^2$. Then $p$ must be even; substituting $p=2k$ shows $q$ also even, contradicting coprimality. Thus the rational numbers have gaps from the viewpoint of limits and geometry.

\begin{workedexample}[Units and normalization]
Compare $\mathrm{spikes}/\mathrm{bin}$ with $\mathrm{spikes}/\mathrm{s}$. If the bin width is $\Delta t$ seconds, the rate estimate is $r=n/\Delta t$ in spikes/s. Changing $\Delta t$ changes the variance and resolution of the estimate even if the underlying experiment is unchanged. This anticipates the bias-variance trade-off in spike-rate estimation.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Well-definedness and the field axioms}
As with integers, the operations on fractions must be checked to be independent of representatives. If $a/b=a'/b'$ (so $ab'=a'b$) then
\[
\frac ab\cdot\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{a'}{b'}\cdot\frac cd=\frac{a'c}{b'd}
\]
are equal because $ac\cdot b'd=(ab')cd=(a'b)cd=a'c\cdot bd$. A similar computation handles addition.

A \emph{field} is a commutative ring with identity $1\ne0$ in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse. In $\mathbb Q$ the inverse of $a/b$ with $a\ne0$ is $b/a$. The field axioms imply all the familiar algebra of fractions; for example, $x\ne0$ and $xy=xz$ give $y=z$ by multiplying with $x^{-1}$.

\section{Order and the Archimedean property}
Call $a/b$ \emph{positive} if $ab>0$; this is independent of representative. Define $x<y$ iff $y-x$ is positive. Then $\mathbb Q$ is an \emph{ordered field}: the order is total, and $x<y$ implies $x+z<y+z$, while $x<y$ and $z>0$ imply $xz<yz$.

\begin{proposition}[Archimedean property]
For every rational $x>0$ and every rational $y$, there is a natural number $n$ with $nx>y$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $x=a/b$ and $y=c/d$ with $a,b,d>0$. Take $n=|c|b+1$. Then $nx=(|c|b+1)a/b\ge|c|+a/b>|c|\ge y$, since $a\ge1$ and $d\ge1$ give $|c|\ge|c|/d\ge c/d$.
\end{proof}
The Archimedean property says there are no infinitely large or infinitely small positive rationals. It justifies statements such as ``choose $N>1/\varepsilon$'' in convergence proofs.

\section{Decimal expansions}
\begin{proposition}
The decimal expansion of a rational number is eventually periodic, and every eventually periodic decimal represents a rational number.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For $p/q$ with $q>0$, long division produces successive remainders in $\{0,\ldots,q-1\}$. By the pigeonhole principle a remainder must repeat within $q+1$ steps, after which the digits repeat. Conversely, if $x=0.\overline{d_1\cdots d_k}$, then $10^kx-x=d_1\cdots d_k$ is an integer, so $x=d_1\cdots d_k/(10^k-1)$; a finite preperiod only shifts and adds a rational.
\end{proof}
Numerical software does not store decimals: it stores binary floating-point numbers, which are rationals with power-of-two denominators. The decimal $0.1$ has an infinite binary expansion and is therefore stored inexactly. This is the origin of the familiar fact that $0.1+0.2\ne0.3$ in double precision, discussed further in Chapter~\ref{ch:numerical}.

\section{Weighted averages and ratio estimators}
Given counts $n_j$ and exposures $T_j>0$, there are two natural rate summaries:
\[
\bar r_{\rm pooled}=\frac{\sum_jn_j}{\sum_jT_j},\qquad
\bar r_{\rm mean}=\frac1J\sum_j\frac{n_j}{T_j}.
\]
The pooled rate is the weighted average of the trial rates $r_j=n_j/T_j$ with weights $w_j=T_j/\sum_kT_k$:
\[
\bar r_{\rm pooled}=\sum_jw_jr_j.
\]
The two summaries coincide when all $T_j$ are equal. Otherwise they answer different questions: the pooled rate is ``spikes per second of recording'', and the mean of rates is ``the typical trial's rate''. Under a constant-rate Poisson model, the pooled rate is the maximum-likelihood estimator; the mean of rates gives more influence to short, noisy trials.

\begin{workedexample}[Simpson's paradox with rates]
Two conditions are recorded on two days. On day 1, condition A yields 20 spikes in 10\,s (2.0 spikes/s) and B yields 210 spikes in 100\,s (2.1 spikes/s). On day 2, A yields 900 spikes in 100\,s (9.0 spikes/s) and B yields 95 spikes in 10\,s (9.5 spikes/s). Condition B has the higher rate on \emph{each} day. Pooled over days, however, A has $920/110\approx8.36$ spikes/s and B has $305/110\approx2.77$ spikes/s. The reversal occurs because A was recorded mostly on the high-rate day and B mostly on the low-rate day. Rational arithmetic is exact here; the paradox lies in which question each ratio answers.
\end{workedexample}
\begin{exercises}
\item Explain what fails if $b=0$ is allowed in a fraction.
\item Calculate the pooled firing rate across a 5-s trial with 20 spikes and a 15-s trial with 30 spikes; compare with the unweighted average of the two trial rates.
\item Prove that between two unequal rationals there is a rational number.
\item Show that the addition of fractions is well defined.
\item Prove that $\mathbb Q$ is not closed under taking square roots of positive elements.
\item Convert $0.1\overline{36}$ to a fraction in lowest terms.
\item Show that $\bar r_{\rm pooled}$ lies between $\min_jr_j$ and $\max_jr_j$.
\item In the Simpson example, compute the mean of daily rates for each condition and explain why it agrees with the day-wise comparison.
\end{exercises}
