\section{From vectors of numbers to functions}
The Euclidean inner product $\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i$ measures agreement between two finite vectors. For sufficiently integrable real functions on an interval, the analogous quantity is
\[\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^b f(t)g(t)\,dt.\]
For complex functions we conjugate the second or first argument according to convention; here we use $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^b f(t)\overline{g(t)}\,dt$, linear in the first argument. The induced norm is $\|f\|_2=(\int |f|^2)^{1/2}$. Signals with finite squared integral belong to the space $L^2$, after identifying functions equal almost everywhere.

\section{Orthogonality and approximation}
Two functions are orthogonal when their inner product is zero. If $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ are orthonormal, the projection of $f$ onto their span is $\sum_j\langle f,e_j\rangle e_j$. The error is orthogonal to the span: this is the same geometry later used in least squares, PCA, and finite-basis spectral expansions.

\section{Measure and probability: what does ``almost everywhere'' mean?}
A measure assigns nonnegative sizes to suitable sets. In the familiar one-dimensional setting, interval length is the basic example. The Lebesgue integral extends Riemann integration and provides a stable theory for limits of integrable functions. ``Almost everywhere'' means except on a set of measure zero. Two functions that differ at finitely many time points can represent the same $L^2$ element, even though pointwise spike events at those instants could be scientifically significant. This reminds us that the choice of mathematical object must fit the observation type.

\section{Generalized signals and impulses}
The ideal Dirac delta $\delta(t-t_0)$ is not an ordinary $L^2$ function. In generalized-function notation its defining property is $\int f(t)\delta(t-t_0)\,dt=f(t_0)$ for suitable test functions. A spike train can be represented formally as $s(t)=\sum_j\delta(t-t_j)$, but practical recordings contain finite-time waveforms and discrete timestamps. Both descriptions are useful, and neither should be confused with the other.

\begin{workedexample}[Projection onto one sinusoid]
On $[0,T]$, let $e(t)=\sqrt{2/T}\cos(2\pi kt/T)$ for a nonzero integer $k$. Its squared integral is one. The coefficient $a=\int_0^T x(t)e(t)\,dt$ determines the best approximation $ae(t)$ to $x(t)$ within this one-dimensional subspace under squared-integral error. This is the geometric core of Fourier analysis.
\end{workedexample}

\section{The Riemann integral and its limits}
For a bounded $f$ on $[a,b]$ and a partition $a=t_0<\cdots<t_n=b$, the upper and lower sums are $U=\sum_k\sup_{[t_{k-1},t_k]}f\,\Delta t_k$ and $L=\sum_k\inf_{[t_{k-1},t_k]}f\,\Delta t_k$. The function is Riemann integrable when $\inf U=\sup L$ over all partitions. Every continuous function on $[a,b]$ is integrable (because it is uniformly continuous there), and so is every monotone function.

The indicator of the rationals on $[0,1]$ (the Dirichlet function) is not Riemann integrable: every subinterval contains rationals and irrationals, so $U=1$ and $L=0$ for every partition. In Lebesgue's theory, the rationals form a set of measure zero and the integral is $0$. This example is artificial, but the deficiency it reveals is serious: Riemann integrals do not behave well under limits.

\section{When can limits and integrals be exchanged?}
Let $f_n(t)=n\,\mathbf 1_{(0,1/n)}(t)$ on $[0,1]$. Then $f_n(t)\to0$ for every $t$, but $\int_0^1f_n=1$ for every $n$. Pointwise convergence does not imply convergence of integrals; the mass escapes into a spike that becomes infinitely narrow, which is precisely how the delta function is approximated.

\begin{theorem}[Uniform convergence]
If $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ (that is, $\sup_t|f_n(t)-f(t)|\to0$) and each $f_n$ is integrable, then $f$ is integrable and $\int f_n\to\int f$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof of the convergence statement]
$\bigl|\int f_n-\int f\bigr|\le\int|f_n-f|\le(b-a)\sup_t|f_n(t)-f(t)|\to0$.
\end{proof}
Lebesgue's \emph{dominated convergence theorem} gives a far more flexible criterion: if $f_n\to f$ almost everywhere and $|f_n|\le g$ for one integrable $g$, then $\int f_n\to\int f$. In the example above no integrable dominating function exists, since $\sup_nf_n(t)\approx1/t$ near zero. Dominated convergence justifies differentiating under the integral sign, exchanging expectation and limits, and many steps in spectral estimation.

\section{Inequalities in inner-product spaces}
\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz]
In any inner-product space, $|\langle f,g\rangle|\le\|f\|\,\|g\|$, with equality iff $f$ and $g$ are linearly dependent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $g=0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise let $c=\langle f,g\rangle/\|g\|^2$ and expand $0\le\|f-cg\|^2=\|f\|^2-|\langle f,g\rangle|^2/\|g\|^2$. Equality holds iff $f=cg$.
\end{proof}
The triangle inequality $\|f+g\|\le\|f\|+\|g\|$ follows by expanding $\|f+g\|^2$ and applying Cauchy--Schwarz. The normalized quantity $\langle f,g\rangle/(\|f\|\|g\|)$ lies in $[-1,1]$ for real functions; applied to centered data vectors it is the Pearson correlation, and applied to cross-spectra across trials it underlies coherence. Cauchy--Schwarz is the reason both are bounded by one.

\begin{theorem}[Pythagoras and Bessel]
If $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ are orthonormal, then for every $f$,
\[
\Bigl\|f-\sum_j\langle f,e_j\rangle e_j\Bigr\|^2=\|f\|^2-\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2,
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2\le\|f\|^2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $p=\sum_j\langle f,e_j\rangle e_j$. Then $\langle f-p,e_j\rangle=0$ for every $j$, so $f-p\perp p$ and $\|f\|^2=\|f-p\|^2+\|p\|^2$, where $\|p\|^2=\sum_j|\langle f,e_j\rangle|^2$ by orthonormality.
\end{proof}
Bessel's inequality says that the energy captured by any orthonormal set of components cannot exceed the total energy. When the orthonormal set is \emph{complete} (its span is dense), equality holds for an infinite sum: this is Parseval's identity, and the Fourier basis is the central example.

\section{Completeness of function spaces}
A normed space is \emph{complete} (a Banach space; a Hilbert space if the norm comes from an inner product) if every Cauchy sequence converges in the space. The continuous functions on $[0,1]$ with the $L^2$ norm are not complete: continuous ramps approximating a step function form a Cauchy sequence whose limit is discontinuous. The space $L^2$ is the completion of this space, which is why Fourier series, least-squares approximation, and stochastic integrals are formulated there. As with $\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R$, completeness is the property that guarantees that approximation procedures have something to converge to.

\section{Convolution}
For integrable $f,g$ on $\mathbb R$, the convolution is $(f\ast g)(t)=\int f(s)g(t-s)\,ds$. It is commutative and associative, and $\|f\ast g\|_1\le\|f\|_1\|g\|_1$. Convolution with a nonnegative kernel of unit integral is a weighted local average; convolving a spike train $\sum_j\delta(t-t_j)$ with a Gaussian kernel $k_\sigma$ gives the familiar smoothed rate $\sum_jk_\sigma(t-t_j)$. The bandwidth $\sigma$ trades temporal resolution for variance, and no choice of $\sigma$ recovers the ``true'' rate without a model of what the rate is.

\begin{workedexample}[Energy of a windowed sinusoid]
Let $x(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_0t)$ on $[0,T]$ with $f_0T$ a positive integer. Then $\|x\|_2^2=A^2T/2$. In the orthonormal basis of the previous example, only the coefficient with $k=f_0T$ is nonzero, and it equals $A\sqrt{T/2}$; Bessel's inequality holds with equality. If $f_0T$ is not an integer, energy spreads across many coefficients, but by Parseval their squared sum is still $\|x\|_2^2$. Leakage redistributes energy; it does not create or destroy it.
\end{workedexample}
