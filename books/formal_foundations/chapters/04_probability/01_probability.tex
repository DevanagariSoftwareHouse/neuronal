\chapter{Probability begins with sets of possible outcomes}\label{ch:probability}
\begin{readergoal}Translate uncertainty into an explicit sample space, events, conditional probabilities, and independence assumptions.\end{readergoal}
\section{Experiments and events}
A random experiment has possible outcomes in a \emph{sample space} $\Omega$. An \emph{event} is a subset $A\subseteq\Omega$. A probability measure $P$ assigns numbers to events with $P(A)\ge0$, $P(\Omega)=1$, and countable additivity across disjoint events. This connects the set operations learned in Chapter~\ref{ch:sets} to the calculus of uncertainty. A model of stochastic spike counts has a different sample space from a model of electrode voltage traces; neither should be silently substituted for the other.

\section{Conditional probability}
When $P(B)>0$, define $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Rearranging gives the multiplication rule $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)P(B)$. If $A$ and $B$ are independent, $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. Independence is a model property, not merely a statement that the observed sample correlation vanishes.

The law of total probability for a partition $(B_j)$ is $P(A)=\sum_jP(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)$. Bayes' theorem follows:
\[P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{\sum_kP(A\mid B_k)P(B_k)}.\]
In Bayesian analysis, a prior over model parameters and a likelihood for observed data yield a posterior. The posterior is conditional on both the likelihood and the prior; a credible interval and a frequentist confidence interval do not have interchangeable interpretations.

\section{Conditioning changes the question}
Two neurons can appear correlated because each varies with movement speed. Conditioning on behavior may reduce this apparent association. But conditioning can also introduce new dependencies, as in collider bias. A scientific claim must specify what was conditioned on, why, and whether the variables were measured accurately. The conditional modeling framework for spike jitter makes this especially explicit \cite{Amarasingham2012}.

\begin{workedexample}[A base-rate effect]
Suppose only 1 percent of candidate events are genuine. A detector identifies 90 percent of genuine events and falsely flags 5 percent of nongenuine events. The probability that a flagged event is genuine is $0.90(0.01)/[0.90(0.01)+0.05(0.99)]\approx0.154$. High sensitivity does not by itself imply high positive predictive value.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Consequences of the axioms}
Every rule of elementary probability follows from three axioms: $P(A)\ge0$, $P(\Omega)=1$, and countable additivity, $P(\bigcup_jA_j)=\sum_jP(A_j)$ for pairwise disjoint events.

\begin{proposition}
For events $A,B$: (i) $P(\varnothing)=0$; (ii) $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$; (iii) if $A\subseteq B$ then $P(A)\le P(B)$ and $P(B\setminus A)=P(B)-P(A)$; (iv) $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$; (v) $P(\bigcup_jA_j)\le\sum_jP(A_j)$ for any countable family (the \emph{union bound}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) Apply additivity to $\Omega,\varnothing,\varnothing,\ldots$: $1=1+\sum P(\varnothing)$. (ii) $\Omega=A\cup A^c$ disjointly. (iii) $B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$ disjointly and $P(B\setminus A)\ge0$. (iv) $A\cup B=A\cup(B\setminus A)$ disjointly and $P(B\setminus A)=P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ by (iii). (v) Set $B_1=A_1$, $B_j=A_j\setminus\bigcup_{i<j}A_i$; the $B_j$ are disjoint with the same union and $B_j\subseteq A_j$.
\end{proof}
The union bound is the mathematical basis of the Bonferroni correction: if each of $m$ tests has false-positive probability at most $\alpha/m$ under its null, the probability of at least one false positive is at most $\alpha$, \emph{whatever} the dependence between tests. Its generality is the source of its conservatism.

\section{Continuity of probability and sigma-algebras}
\begin{proposition}[Continuity from below and above]
If $A_1\subseteq A_2\subseteq\cdots$ then $P(\bigcup_nA_n)=\lim_nP(A_n)$. If $A_1\supseteq A_2\supseteq\cdots$ then $P(\bigcap_nA_n)=\lim_nP(A_n)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For increasing events, write the union as the disjoint union of $A_1,A_2\setminus A_1,A_3\setminus A_2,\ldots$; countable additivity gives $P(\bigcup A_n)=\lim_N\sum_{n\le N}P(A_n\setminus A_{n-1})=\lim_NP(A_N)$. The decreasing case follows by complements.
\end{proof}
On uncountable sample spaces, such as the set of all voltage traces, one cannot in general assign probabilities to \emph{every} subset consistently. Probabilities are assigned to a \emph{$\sigma$-algebra} $\mathcal F$: a collection of subsets containing $\Omega$ and closed under complements and countable unions. The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$ is a \emph{probability space}. The Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb R$, generated by intervals, contains every set one meets in practice. The restriction is technical but explains the word ``measurable'' attached to random variables.

\section{Independence of several events}
Events $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ are \emph{mutually independent} if $P(\bigcap_{i\in J}A_i)=\prod_{i\in J}P(A_i)$ for every subset $J$ of indices. Pairwise independence is weaker.

\begin{workedexample}[Pairwise but not mutually independent]
Toss two fair coins. Let $A$ = ``first is heads'', $B$ = ``second is heads'', $C$ = ``the coins agree''. Each has probability $1/2$, and each pair intersects in a single outcome of probability $1/4$, so the events are pairwise independent. But $A\cap B\subseteq C$, so $P(A\cap B\cap C)=1/4\ne1/8$. In neural terms: three binary units can be pairwise uncorrelated while their joint activity is fully constrained, which is why pairwise statistics cannot certify the absence of higher-order structure.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Conditional probability as a new probability measure}
For fixed $B$ with $P(B)>0$, the function $A\mapsto P(A\mid B)$ satisfies all three axioms; it is a probability measure on the same $\sigma$-algebra, concentrated on $B$. Every theorem of probability therefore holds conditionally. In particular, \emph{conditional independence} of $A$ and $C$ given $B$ means $P(A\cap C\mid B)=P(A\mid B)P(C\mid B)$, and it neither implies nor is implied by unconditional independence.

\begin{proposition}[Chain rule]
If $P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1})>0$, then
\[
P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_n)=P(A_1)\,P(A_2\mid A_1)\cdots P(A_n\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{n-1}).
\]
\end{proposition}
The proof is induction on the multiplication rule. The chain rule is the probability of a sequence written as a product of conditional probabilities of each step given the past, the form taken by every likelihood for time series and spike trains (Chapter~\ref{ch:points}).

\begin{workedexample}[Conditioning creates dependence]
Two neurons fire independently with probability $0.1$ each in a bin. Suppose a recording is retained only if at least one of them fires (a collider). Given retention, $P(\text{both}\mid\text{retained})=0.01/0.19\approx0.053$, while $P(\text{first}\mid\text{retained})=P(\text{second}\mid\text{retained})=0.1/0.19\approx0.526$, whose product is $0.277$. The neurons are now strongly \emph{negatively} dependent: knowing one did not fire guarantees that the other did. Selection on an outcome manufactures structure.
\end{workedexample}
