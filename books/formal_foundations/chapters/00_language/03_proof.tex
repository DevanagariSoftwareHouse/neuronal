\section{The anatomy of an argument}
A proof begins with an exact statement and a list of assumptions. It proceeds through steps justified by a definition, an axiom, or an earlier result. The conclusion is valid \emph{conditional on those assumptions}. Mathematical evidence and empirical evidence answer different questions: a covariance matrix is positive semidefinite by construction, but a measured covariance need not describe only neuronal interactions.

\section{Direct proof and proof by contrapositive}
We say an integer $n$ is even if $n=2k$ for some integer $k$. To prove that the sum of two even integers is even, write $a=2k$ and $b=2m$. Then $a+b=2(k+m)$; since $k+m$ is an integer, the sum is even. Notice the explicit use of the definition. An assertion such as ``the result is obvious'' hides exactly the step a beginner may need.

For the contrapositive method, consider: if $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even. Prove instead that odd $n=2k+1$ implies $n^2=4k(k+1)+1$, which is odd. This avoids assuming the conclusion.

\section{Contradiction, induction, and counterexamples}
Proof by contradiction begins with the negation of the proposed conclusion, then derives an impossibility. The classic irrationality argument for $\sqrt2$ assumes $\sqrt2=a/b$ with coprime integers $a,b$, derives that both must be even, and contradicts coprimality. We shall revisit this when constructing the real numbers.

A single valid counterexample refutes a universal proposition. To refute ``every high-power spectral peak is neuronal,'' one can simulate a sinusoidal electrical artifact. To refute ``correlation implies coupling,'' one can simulate two independently noisy signals influenced by a common source. These examples do not show that neuronal oscillations or coupling never occur; they refute the unrestricted inference.

\section{Existence, uniqueness, and identifiability}
Three questions recur throughout this book: Does the object exist? Is it uniquely determined? Can we infer it from the observations? The first two are mathematical; the third also depends on a measurement model. For example, different neuronal current distributions may produce the same measured extracellular potential, so a valid forward model does not guarantee a unique inverse solution.

\begin{proposition}If $A$ and $B$ are subsets of a fixed universe $U$, then $A=B$ if and only if both $A\subseteq B$ and $B\subseteq A$.\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}If $A=B$, either inclusion follows immediately. Conversely, the first inclusion implies every element of $A$ belongs to $B$, and the second implies every element of $B$ belongs to $A$. The two sets therefore have exactly the same elements.\end{proof}

\section{Writing a proof: from scratch work to final form}
Proofs are discovered backwards and written forwards. In scratch work one starts from the desired conclusion, unpacks its definitions, and asks what would suffice. The written proof reverses the direction: it begins with the hypotheses and proceeds by justified steps to the conclusion. A useful discipline is the \emph{two-column check}: for every line of a draft proof, name the definition, axiom, earlier result, or logical rule that justifies it. A line without a justification is either a gap or an unstated assumption.

Good proofs also announce their structure. Phrases such as ``we argue by contradiction'', ``we proceed by induction on $n$'', or ``it suffices to show'' tell the reader which logical form is being used. The phrase ``without loss of generality'' (WLOG) is legitimate only when the omitted cases genuinely follow by symmetry; it should never hide a case that behaves differently.

\section{Proof by cases and the pigeonhole principle}
When a hypothesis is a disjunction, or when the domain splits naturally, one may prove the conclusion separately in each case.

\begin{proposition}
For every integer $n$, the integer $n^2+n$ is even.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Either $n$ is even or $n$ is odd. If $n=2k$, then $n^2+n=2k(2k+1)$, which is even. If $n=2k+1$, then $n^2+n=(2k+1)(2k+2)=2(2k+1)(k+1)$, which is even. The two cases are exhaustive.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Pigeonhole principle]
If $m$ objects are placed into $k$ boxes and $m>k$, then some box contains at least two objects. More generally, some box contains at least $\lceil m/k\rceil$ objects.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Suppose, for contradiction, that every box contains at most $\lceil m/k\rceil-1$ objects. Since $\lceil m/k\rceil-1<m/k$, the total number of objects is at most $k(\lceil m/k\rceil-1)<k\cdot m/k=m$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}
The principle has a direct data-analysis reading: if 1001 spikes are recorded in a 1-second window binned at 1\,ms, at least one bin contains two spikes. A binary (0/1) spike representation at that resolution is then necessarily lossy.

\section{Strong induction and well ordering}
Ordinary induction proves $P(n+1)$ from $P(n)$. \emph{Strong induction} allows the use of all previous cases: if $P(0)$ holds and $\bigl(P(0)\land\cdots\land P(n)\bigr)\Rightarrow P(n+1)$ for every $n$, then $P(n)$ holds for every $n\in\mathbb N_0$. It is equivalent to ordinary induction applied to the statement $Q(n)=P(0)\land\cdots\land P(n)$. A further equivalent form is the \emph{well-ordering principle}: every nonempty subset of $\mathbb N_0$ has a least element.

\begin{proposition}
Every integer $n\ge2$ is a product of one or more primes.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By strong induction. The integer $2$ is prime. Let $n>2$ and assume every integer $m$ with $2\le m<n$ is a product of primes. If $n$ is prime we are done. Otherwise $n=ab$ with $2\le a,b<n$; by hypothesis both $a$ and $b$ are products of primes, and so is their product.
\end{proof}

\section{Common errors in purported proofs}
Learning to \emph{audit} arguments is as important as learning to write them. The following patterns recur.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \emph{Proof by example.} Checking a universal claim for several values does not prove it. The polynomial $n^2+n+41$ is prime for $n=0,1,\ldots,39$, but $40^2+40+41=41^2$.
\item \emph{Circular reasoning.} A step that silently uses the conclusion. ``The estimator is unbiased because its expected value equals the parameter'' restates rather than proves.
\item \emph{Division by zero.} Cancelling a factor that may vanish. From $ax=ay$ one may conclude $x=y$ only when $a\ne0$.
\item \emph{Wrong quantifier order.} Choosing an $N$ that depends on $n$ when the definition requires $N$ to depend only on $\varepsilon$.
\item \emph{Unjustified interchange of limits.} Exchanging a limit with a sum, integral, or derivative without a theorem that allows it. Chapter~\ref{ch:functionspaces} gives counterexamples.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{workedexample}[Auditing a methods argument]
Claim: ``Because the shuffled-control coherence is lower than the observed coherence on every one of 20 sessions, coherence reflects genuine coupling.'' Audit: (i) the premise concerns 20 sessions, while ``genuine coupling'' is a claim about a mechanism; (ii) shuffling trials destroys trial-locked common input as well as coupling, so the control does not separate the two hypotheses; (iii) consistency across sessions shows reproducibility of the comparison, not uniqueness of its explanation. The argument is a valid rejection of the shuffled null, and an invalid inference to a specific mechanism.
\end{workedexample}
\begin{exercises}
\item Prove that the intersection of two subsets is contained in each subset.
\item Give two distinct functions with the same value at zero.
\item Distinguish ``the algorithm converges'' from ``the result estimates the intended biological quantity.''
\item Write a three-line counterexample to ``a zero Pearson correlation implies independence.''
\item Prove by cases that $|ab|=|a||b|$ for real numbers $a,b$.
\item Use the pigeonhole principle to show that among any 13 people two were born in the same month.
\item Prove by strong induction that every amount of at least 12 cents can be paid with 4- and 5-cent coins.
\item Find the error: ``Let $a=b$. Then $a^2-b^2=ab-b^2$, so $(a+b)(a-b)=b(a-b)$, hence $a+b=b$ and $2=1$.''
\item Show that the well-ordering principle implies ordinary induction.
\end{exercises}
