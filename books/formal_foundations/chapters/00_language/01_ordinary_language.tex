\chapter{From ordinary English to mathematical statements}\label{ch:english}
\begin{readergoal}Read, question, and translate statements before manipulating symbols. No mathematical prerequisite is assumed.\end{readergoal}
\section{Why begin with sentences?}
A scientist looks at an experiment and makes claims: ``this animal ran faster,'' ``these spikes followed the stimulus,'' or ``these electrodes recorded the same rhythm.'' At first, the claims are expressed in ordinary language. Mathematics begins when we decide exactly what would make a claim true, how it could be false, and what follows from it. The symbols are a shorthand for decisions already made in words. Learning a formula without learning its grammar is like memorizing a sentence in a language one cannot read.

Consider ``every recorded neuron fired.'' Does this refer to every neuron in the brain, every electrode channel, or every neuron retained after spike sorting? The statement is incomplete until we specify its domain of discourse. Consider ``some neurons were silent.'' The word \emph{some} means \emph{at least one}; it does not exclude the possibility that many neurons were silent. The word \emph{or} in standard mathematics ordinarily permits both alternatives. When a manuscript says ``the signal is oscillatory,'' we must also decide what counts as an oscillation. A label is not yet an operational test.

\section{Statements, questions, definitions, and hypotheses}
A \emph{proposition} is a declarative statement to which a truth value can be assigned, given a specified interpretation. ``Two is even'' is a proposition. ``Is two even?'' is a question, while ``Let $n$ be the number of spikes'' is an instruction introducing notation. ``For every $t$ in the recording interval, $x(t)>0$'' becomes a proposition once $x$ and the recording interval are fixed. An \emph{open sentence} such as ``$n$ is even'' has a free variable and does not yet assert anything about a particular number.

A \emph{definition} establishes how a term or symbol will be used; a \emph{hypothesis} asserts a fact that may or may not hold; a \emph{theorem} is a proposition established from previously stated assumptions by valid reasoning. In applied science we must further distinguish a mathematical theorem, which follows within a model, from an empirical assertion about the world, which requires observations.

\section{Translating ordinary English}
Let $U$ be the set of units retained after quality control. Introduce the predicate $F(u)$ for ``unit $u$ fired in a designated 100-ms window.'' Then ``every retained unit fired'' becomes
\[ \forall u\in U,\ F(u). \]
``At least one retained unit did not fire'' becomes $\exists u\in U$ such that $\neg F(u)$. These are logical negations of one another. ``No unit fired'' instead means $\forall u\in U,\neg F(u)$. Notice the difference between a negative statement about \emph{all} units and a statement that \emph{not all} units fired.

In data analysis, ``higher gamma amplitude when theta phase is near its peak'' must be turned into an explicit comparison: define phase bins, summarize amplitudes, select a test statistic, and specify a null hypothesis. This transition from sentence to mathematical object is the beginning of sound methodology.

\section{A claim is only as good as its scope}
Suppose 40 of 50 detected units increase firing rate during a task. It is legitimate to say ``80 percent of these detected units increased.'' It is not automatically legitimate to say ``80 percent of neurons in this brain area increased.'' Electrode sampling, sorting errors, and selection rules change the population to which a claim refers. We will repeatedly separate \emph{a set of observed objects}, \emph{a mathematical model}, and \emph{a physiological population}.

\begin{workedexample}[The ambiguity of ``only'']
Compare ``only neurons in CA1 show a theta rhythm'' with ``neurons in CA1 show only a theta rhythm.'' The first restricts the location of units exhibiting theta; the second restricts the rhythms observed in CA1 units. Write down what observation would contradict each statement. The two claims can disagree even on the same recording.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Vagueness, ambiguity, and operational definitions}
Ordinary language tolerates two different kinds of imprecision. A word is \emph{ambiguous} when it has several distinct meanings (``rate'' may mean spikes per second or spikes per trial). A word is \emph{vague} when its meaning has no sharp boundary (``high'' power, ``many'' spikes, ``fast'' ripples). Mathematics removes ambiguity by choosing one meaning, and removes vagueness by choosing a threshold or a comparison. Both choices are decisions, not discoveries.

An \emph{operational definition} replaces a vague predicate by a rule that any analyst could apply to the same data and obtain the same verdict. For example, ``the trial contains a ripple'' may be replaced by: ``the 150--250\,Hz band-passed envelope exceeds the session median by five median absolute deviations for at least 15 consecutive milliseconds.'' This rule is reproducible, but it is not thereby \emph{correct}: other reasonable thresholds yield other sets of ripples. A sound report states the rule and examines how conclusions change when the rule changes.

\begin{remark}
A precise definition can be scientifically poor. Precision guarantees that two readers agree on what was computed; it does not guarantee that the computed object corresponds to the physiological phenomenon that motivated the word.
\end{remark}

\section{The logical skeleton of a scientific sentence}
Most claims in a methods or results section have a recurring skeleton:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item a \emph{domain}: the objects being talked about (units, trials, sessions, animals);
\item a \emph{predicate} applied to those objects (``increased its rate'', ``was phase locked'');
\item a \emph{quantifier} stating how many objects satisfy the predicate (all, some, most, a fraction);
\item a \emph{condition} restricting when the claim applies (``during running'', ``after stimulation'');
\item a \emph{standard of evidence} by which the predicate was judged (a test, a threshold, an estimate with uncertainty).
\end{enumerate}
Making each component explicit is the first step toward mathematical form. Consider ``place cells remap in the novel environment.'' Its domain is presumably units classified as place cells by some criterion; its predicate is a change of firing field; its quantifier is unstated (all? most? a significant fraction?); its condition is exposure to a novel arena; and its standard of evidence must specify how two firing maps are compared. A sentence missing any component is not false; it is \emph{incomplete}.

\section{Words that hide quantifiers}
Several everyday words carry hidden logical structure:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item ``\emph{any}'' usually means \emph{every} in positive statements (``any unit can be recorded'') but \emph{at least one} in negative or interrogative ones (``no unit showed any change'');
\item ``\emph{unless}'' means \emph{if not}: ``$P$ unless $Q$'' is $\neg Q\Rightarrow P$;
\item ``\emph{only if}'' marks a necessary condition: ``$P$ only if $Q$'' is $P\Rightarrow Q$, not $Q\Rightarrow P$;
\item ``\emph{the}'' presupposes existence and uniqueness: ``the peak frequency'' assumes that exactly one peak exists;
\item ``\emph{typically}'' and ``\emph{tends to}'' are statistical statements and require a distribution, not a quantifier.
\end{itemize}
The definite article deserves special attention. Writing ``let $f^\ast$ be the peak frequency of the spectrum'' is meaningful only after establishing that the maximum exists and is attained at a single point. If two frequencies attain the same maximal power, the phrase ``the peak'' has no referent, and software will silently pick one (often the first index), which is an arbitrary convention rather than a property of the data.

\begin{workedexample}[Rewriting a results sentence]
Original: ``Interneurons fire before pyramidal cells.'' A careful rewrite fixes each component. Let $I$ be units classified as putative interneurons by waveform width below $0.4$\,ms; let $P$ be the remaining units; for a pair $(i,p)\in I\times P$ let $\delta(i,p)$ be the lag of the peak of the cross-correlogram within $\pm10$\,ms. The quantitative claim might be: ``for 68\% of pairs in $I\times P$, $\delta(i,p)<0$, compared with 50\% expected under a jitter null.'' The rewritten sentence is longer, but now a reader can check it, reproduce it, and disagree with it in a specific way.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Model statements versus world statements}
A model statement has the form ``\emph{if} the data were generated by mechanism $M$ with parameters $\theta$, \emph{then} quantity $Q$ would have property $R$.'' It may be proved. A world statement has the form ``quantity $Q$ in this recording has property $R$.'' It is supported or contradicted by observations. Much confusion in quantitative neuroscience comes from presenting a model statement in the grammar of a world statement. ``The spectrum has a 1/$f$ background'' could mean that a fitted power law describes the estimate well over a range, or it could mean that the generative process has a specific scale-free structure. The first is descriptive; the second requires additional argument. Throughout this book we mark which kind of statement is being made.
\begin{exercises}
\item Translate ``not every trial contains a ripple'' into a quantified sentence and simplify its negation.
\item Explain why ``all spikes occur in bursts'' can be vacuously true for an empty spike train.
\item Identify the observational unit in each claim: neuron, trial, event, animal, and session.
\item Write one empirically falsifiable claim about coherence and one purely mathematical definition of coherence.
\item Rewrite ``$P$ unless $Q$'' and ``$P$ only if $Q$'' as implications, and give a neuroscience sentence of each form.
\item Give an operational definition of ``burst'' for a spike train, and name two alternative definitions that would classify some spikes differently.
\item Find a sentence in a paper you have read that uses ``the'' for an object whose uniqueness was not established. Rewrite it.
\item Decompose ``most hippocampal neurons were modulated by speed'' into domain, predicate, quantifier, condition, and standard of evidence.
\item Classify each of the following as a model statement or a world statement: ``a Poisson process has Fano factor one''; ``these units had Fano factors near one''; ``cortical neurons fire like Poisson processes.''
\end{exercises}
