\chapter{Spike-train GLMs, resampling, and conditional hypotheses}\label{ch:glm}
\begin{readergoal}Build spike prediction models and valid null tests from conditional probabilities instead of selecting statistics first.\end{readergoal}
\section{A Poisson generalized linear model}
A GLM relates the expected count to covariates through a link function. For binned counts $n_k$ over exposure $\Delta_k$, one specification is
\[n_k\mid x_k\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu_k),\qquad\log\mu_k=\log\Delta_k+\beta_0+x_k^\top\beta.\]
The offset $\log\Delta_k$ ensures that changing bin exposure has the correct interpretation in rate units. Under this model, $\lambda_k=\exp(\beta_0+x_k^\top\beta)$ is the conditional event rate. For sufficiently fine bins a Bernoulli model with appropriate link can also be used, but the likelihood assumptions differ.

\section{History filters and coupling}
A point-process GLM can augment predictors with spike-history bases or other units' recent spikes. Negative recent-self-history weights may express refractoriness; positive interactions may express predictive coupling. The estimated coefficients depend on basis choice, observation artifacts, unobserved common inputs, and regularization. Coupling filters are model parameters and not direct measurements of synaptic transmission.

\section{Model assessment rather than fit alone}
Inspect residual or time-rescaled-event structure, held-out log-likelihood, calibration, stability across bin widths, and sensitivity to covariates. Time-rescaling transforms integrated intensities between consecutive events; a correctly specified conditional-intensity model yields rescaled intervals with a known reference distribution. Failure suggests misspecification but does not identify the correct alternative automatically.

\section{Jitter and conditional resampling}
A resampling null model may fix the spike count inside each jitter window and randomize event locations within specified allowed regions. Exactness of conditional tests depends on the null distribution used for resampling. Generic ``jitter the spikes'' is not a statistical procedure until its preserved quantities, randomized quantities, boundary handling, and sampling distribution are fully specified. Conditional modeling clarifies the hypotheses supported by interval jitter and related tests \cite{Amarasingham2012}.

If $T_{\rm obs}$ is a test statistic and $T_1^*,\ldots,T_B^*$ are exchangeable null replicates under the test construction, a common Monte Carlo tail estimate is
\[\hat p=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^B\mathbf1\{T_b^*\ge T_{\rm obs}\}}{B+1}.\]
The plus-one convention avoids zero estimated $p$-values, but validity also requires the simulation to represent the intended null.

\begin{workedexample}[What is preserved?]
Suppose one window has three spikes. A conditional jitter method preserving this count cannot test whether the process increased its number of spikes in that window; it can test aspects of fine timing within the window. Rejection must be interpreted relative to preserved coarse structure, not as evidence for a particular synapse.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Concavity of the Poisson GLM log-likelihood}
With $\log\mu_k=\log\Delta_k+x_k^\top\beta$ (absorbing $\beta_0$ into $\beta$ with a constant regressor), the log-likelihood is
\[
\ell(\beta)=\sum_k\bigl[n_kx_k^\top\beta-\Delta_ke^{x_k^\top\beta}\bigr]+\text{const}.
\]
\begin{proposition}
The gradient and Hessian are
\[
\nabla\ell(\beta)=\sum_k(n_k-\mu_k)x_k,\qquad\nabla^2\ell(\beta)=-\sum_k\mu_kx_kx_k^\top=-X^\top WX,
\]
with $W=\diag(\mu_k)$. Hence $\ell$ is concave, and strictly concave if $X$ has full column rank.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate term by term. For any $v$, $v^\top\nabla^2\ell\,v=-\sum_k\mu_k(x_k^\top v)^2\le0$, with equality only if $Xv=0$.
\end{proof}
Concavity means every local maximum is global, and Newton's method converges reliably from reasonable starting points. The Newton step can be written as a weighted least-squares problem with working response $\tilde y_k=x_k^\top\beta+(n_k-\mu_k)/\mu_k$: this is \emph{iteratively reweighted least squares}. The MLE may fail to exist if the data are \emph{separable}, for example if a regressor is positive in every bin with spikes and zero otherwise: the likelihood then increases without bound as the coefficient grows. Regularization (a ridge penalty $-\frac\alpha2\|\beta\|^2$ keeps concavity) resolves this, at the cost of bias.

\section{Basis functions for filters}
A stimulus or history filter of length $L$ bins has $L$ free coefficients. Representing it as $h=Bc$ with a basis $B\in\mathbb R^{L\times J}$, $J\ll L$, reduces parameters and imposes smoothness. Raised-cosine bumps with logarithmically stretched time are common for history filters because they give fine resolution at short lags (refractoriness) and coarse resolution at long lags. The basis choice is a prior: features not representable in the span of $B$ cannot be estimated, and the apparent shape of a fitted filter partly reflects the basis.

\section{Goodness of fit with time rescaling}
Given a fitted conditional intensity $\hat\lambda(t\mid\mathcal H_t)$ and spike times $t_k$, compute $z_k=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_k}\hat\lambda(s\mid\mathcal H_s)\,ds$ and $u_k=1-e^{-z_k}$. If the model is correct, the $u_k$ are iid uniform. A KS plot compares their sorted values with uniform quantiles, with approximate 95\% bands $\pm1.36/\sqrt K$ for $K$ spikes. Two practical caveats apply. In discrete time, the integral must be computed with a correction for the bin width, or the test rejects correct models at high rates. And with many spikes the bands become narrow enough that every model is rejected; the KS plot is then more useful as a diagnostic of \emph{where} the model fails than as a binary test.

\section{Validity of Monte Carlo tests}
\begin{proposition}
Suppose that under $H_0$ the values $T_{\rm obs},T_1^\ast,\ldots,T_B^\ast$ are exchangeable and almost surely distinct. Then $P_{H_0}(\hat p\le\alpha)\le\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in(0,1)$, where $\hat p=(1+\#\{b:T_b^\ast\ge T_{\rm obs}\})/(B+1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By exchangeability, the rank of $T_{\rm obs}$ among the $B+1$ values is uniformly distributed on $\{1,\ldots,B+1\}$. The event $\hat p\le\alpha$ says that $T_{\rm obs}$ is among the $\lfloor\alpha(B+1)\rfloor$ largest, which has probability $\lfloor\alpha(B+1)\rfloor/(B+1)\le\alpha$.
\end{proof}
The hypothesis is exchangeability \emph{of the observed data with the surrogates under the null}. Interval jitter achieves it exactly when the null is ``given the counts in each window, spike positions within windows are uniform and independent'': the observed train is then one draw from the same conditional distribution that generates the surrogates. Shuffling procedures that do not have this property yield $p$-values whose validity is unknown.

\begin{workedexample}[A coupling filter that is really a common input]
Simulate two neurons that are independent given a shared slow input $s(t)$, with intensities $\exp(a+s(t))$ for each. Fit a GLM for neuron 2 that includes neuron 1's recent spikes but omits $s$. The fitted coupling filter from 1 to 2 is positive at short lags, because neuron 1's spikes are a noisy proxy for $s$. Including an estimate of $s$, for instance a smoothed population rate or the LFP, shrinks the coupling filter towards zero. The coefficient measured the predictive value of neuron 1 given the regressors \emph{in the model}; it changes whenever the model changes.
\end{workedexample}
