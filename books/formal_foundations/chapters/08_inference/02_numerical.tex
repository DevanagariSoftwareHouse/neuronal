\section{A formula is not yet an algorithm}
A mathematical expression can define an exact quantity while computer code approximates it. Floating-point arithmetic rounds values and is not always associative: $(a+b)+c$ and $a+(b+c)$ can differ. An apparently exact bin count computed by $\lfloor T/\Delta\rfloor$ can be off by one if $T/\Delta$ is represented just below an integer. Implementations must state endpoint conventions and test boundary cases rather than rely on a universal ad hoc epsilon.

\section{Conditioning and algorithmic stability}
A well-posed problem has a solution that exists, is unique, and depends continuously on the data in an appropriate sense. An ill-conditioned problem may magnify small perturbations even under perfect implementation. A numerically unstable algorithm may magnify errors beyond what conditioning requires. Computing least-squares coefficients via QR or SVD is typically more stable than directly inverting $X^\top X$. Regularization stabilizes some inverse problems by choosing a constrained or penalized solution, at the cost of explicit modeling assumptions.

\section{Memory and complexity}
A dense sample covariance among $N$ neurons requires $O(N^2)$ storage, while a full matrix decomposition can be computationally expensive for large $N$. When sample count $T$ is much smaller than $N$, methods operating on a $T\times T$ Gram matrix can be more efficient. Signal operations can frequently be evaluated chunk by chunk, although filtering chunks requires state continuity or overlap handling. Memory mapping avoids loading full arrays but does not eliminate I/O costs.

\section{Testing the mathematical contract}
Good numerical tests use known synthetic inputs: a sinusoid with known frequency for PSD estimation; a complex exponential for phase operations; a Poisson simulation for count statistics; a low-rank signal with controlled noise for PCA; and a known VAR for Granger analysis. Property-based tests can verify constraints such as covariance symmetry, nonnegative variance, $\mathrm{PLV}\in[0,1]$, and consistency of concatenated block processing with full-array processing within a stated tolerance.

\section{Scientific metadata as part of the result}
An estimate needs sampling rate, time origin, measurement units, reference configuration, filtering parameters, electrode and trial identifiers, quality masks, random seeds, and software versions. Metadata are part of the mathematical map between the array and its real-world interpretation. A floating-point matrix detached from its coordinates can be mathematically valid yet scientifically unusable.

\section{Floating-point error analysis}
The standard model of floating-point arithmetic states that for each basic operation $\circ\in\{+,-,\times,/\}$, $\mathrm{fl}(a\circ b)=(a\circ b)(1+\delta)$ with $|\delta|\le u=\epsilon_{\rm mach}/2$. Errors in a long computation are analysed by propagating these factors.

\begin{proposition}[Error of recursive summation]
Computing $s=\sum_{i=1}^nx_i$ left to right gives $\hat s$ with $|\hat s-s|\le(n-1)u\sum_i|x_i|+O(u^2)$.
\end{proposition}
The bound grows linearly in $n$ and with $\sum|x_i|$, not $|s|$: summing many terms of mixed sign whose total nearly cancels gives large \emph{relative} error. Pairwise (tree) summation reduces the factor $n-1$ to $\lceil\log_2n\rceil$, and compensated (Kahan) summation reduces it to a constant.

\paragraph{Catastrophic cancellation.} Subtracting two nearly equal rounded numbers exposes their rounding errors. The one-pass variance formula $\frac1n\sum x_i^2-\bar x^2$ suffers from this when the mean is large relative to the standard deviation: for a signal with mean $10^4\,\mu$V and standard deviation $1\,\mu$V, the two terms agree to eight digits and the computed variance retains only about eight correct digits in double precision, or none in single precision. The two-pass formula $\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$, or Welford's streaming update, avoids the problem.

\section{Backward stability}
An algorithm is \emph{backward stable} if its computed answer is the exact answer for slightly perturbed data, with perturbation of order $u$. Combined with the condition number, this gives the rule of thumb
\[
\text{relative forward error}\lesssim\kappa\times\text{backward error}\approx\kappa u.
\]
Householder QR and the SVD are backward stable for least squares, so the error is governed by $\kappa(X)$. Solving the normal equations is governed by $\kappa(X)^2$. For $\kappa(X)=10^6$ in double precision, QR may deliver about ten correct digits and the normal equations about four.

Estimating cost before running a full analysis is part of numerical design.

\section{Randomness and reproducibility}
Pseudorandom number generators are deterministic sequences initialized by a seed. Recording the seed and generator type makes a resampling analysis exactly reproducible. Monte Carlo $p$-values carry their own sampling error: with $B$ surrogates and true $p$, the estimate has standard error about $\sqrt{p(1-p)/B}$. Near $p=0.05$, $B=1000$ gives a standard error near $0.007$, so a reported $p=0.048$ is not distinguishable from $0.052$. When a decision depends on the third decimal, increase $B$.

\begin{workedexample}[Counting bins without floating-point surprises]
Spike times are stored in seconds and binned at $\Delta=0.001$\,s. Computing the bin index as \texttt{floor(t/0.001)} for $t=0.003$ may return $2$, because neither $0.003$ nor $0.001$ is exactly representable and their quotient can round to $2.9999999999999996$. Storing times as integer sample counts at the acquisition rate, or computing indices as \texttt{floor(round(t*fs)/ (fs*Delta))} with integer arithmetic, removes the ambiguity. The mathematical definition of a bin is exact; only its floating-point implementation is fragile.
\end{workedexample}
\begin{exercises}
\item Explain why zero-padding an FFT does not improve physical spectral resolution.
\item Design a test for chunked FIR filtering that detects boundary discontinuities.
\item List the metadata necessary to interpret a Hilbert-envelope moment series from 10-second windows.
\item Show that the two-pass variance formula is algebraically equal to the one-pass formula, and explain why they differ numerically.
\item Implement Welford's update $\bar x_k=\bar x_{k-1}+(x_k-\bar x_{k-1})/k$, $M_k=M_{k-1}+(x_k-\bar x_{k-1})(x_k-\bar x_k)$, and verify that $M_n/(n-1)$ is the sample variance.
\item Estimate the time and memory needed to compute a $2048$-point FFT spectrogram of a 2-hour, 384-channel recording at 2.5\,kHz with 50\% overlap.
\item How many surrogates are needed for a Monte Carlo $p$-value near $0.01$ to have standard error $0.001$?
\end{exercises}
