\chapter{State-space dynamics, neural manifolds, and rotations}\label{ch:dynamics}
\begin{readergoal}Connect dynamical evolution, latent inference, and geometric structure while keeping descriptive and mechanistic claims separate.\end{readergoal}
\section{A state is more than a snapshot}
A discrete linear dynamical system has $z_{t+1}=Az_t+Bu_t+w_t$ and observations $y_t=Cz_t+v_t$. The hidden state $z_t$ evolves according to a transition rule while observations mix it with noise. If the model is linear Gaussian, Kalman filtering estimates the posterior state sequentially. Smoothing uses future as well as past observations and is not equivalent to an online estimate.

\section{Continuous dynamics and stability}
A continuous linear system obeys $\dot z=Az+Bu(t)$. The eigenvalues of $A$ govern local behavior: negative real parts give asymptotic decay for unforced linear systems, while complex eigenvalues generate damped rotational components. More generally $\dot z=f(z,u,t)$ can represent nonlinear dynamics. Stable equilibria and attractors are properties of the chosen model; inferring them from noisy, partial observations requires care.

\section{jPCA and rotational population trajectories}
Principal-component analysis selects directions of large variance, not necessarily directions displaying dynamics. The jPCA approach first reduces dimensionality, then fits an approximately skew-symmetric linear flow for the derivatives of the reduced trajectories, highlighting rotations \cite{Churchland2012}. The existence of a rotational projection is a description of population activity under chosen preprocessing; attributing that rotation to a particular recurrent mechanism requires additional evidence.

\section{Neural manifolds and coordinate nonuniqueness}
A low-dimensional manifold is a set locally parameterized by a smaller number of coordinates than its ambient dimension. If observations obey $y=g(z)$ with $z\in\mathbb R^d$, many coordinate systems for $z$ can describe the same observed trajectories. Thus latent axes may not have unique biological labels. A fitted low-dimensional model can be useful for decoding even when its coordinates are not uniquely identifiable. Reviews of movement-related manifolds emphasize dynamical and task constraints \cite{Gallego2017}.

\section{Nonlinear latent models}
Sequential autoencoders and recurrent neural networks can infer nonlinear latent dynamics from single trials, under training objectives and architecture assumptions. Models such as LFADS use latent generative dynamics for population spike data. Flexible models can also overfit. Appropriate controls include held-out likelihood, behavior decoding, simulation-based identifiability tests, and stability across recording sessions.

\begin{workedexample}[A two-dimensional rotation]
Let $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega\\\omega&0\end{bmatrix}$. Then $\dot z=Az$ has solutions $z_1(t)=R\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and $z_2(t)=R\sin(\omega t+\phi)$. The trajectory is a circle of radius $R$. A change of orthonormal coordinates rotates the picture but leaves its essential frequency unchanged.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Solving linear systems of differential equations}
For $\dot z=Az$ with $z(0)=z_0$, the solution is $z(t)=e^{tA}z_0$, where the matrix exponential $e^{tA}=\sum_{k\ge0}t^kA^k/k!$ converges for every $A$. If $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ is diagonalizable, $e^{tA}=Ve^{t\Lambda}V^{-1}$ and each mode evolves as $e^{\lambda_jt}$. A complex pair $\lambda=\alpha\pm i\omega$ produces oscillations at angular frequency $\omega$ with envelope $e^{\alpha t}$. Hence:
\begin{theorem}
The equilibrium $z=0$ of $\dot z=Az$ is asymptotically stable if and only if every eigenvalue of $A$ has negative real part. For the discrete system $z_{t+1}=Az_t$, the condition is that every eigenvalue has modulus less than one.
\end{theorem}
For nonlinear systems $\dot z=f(z)$, the \emph{linearization} at an equilibrium $z^\ast$ ($f(z^\ast)=0$) uses the Jacobian $J=Df(z^\ast)$. If every eigenvalue of $J$ has negative real part, $z^\ast$ is locally asymptotically stable; if one has positive real part, it is unstable; eigenvalues on the imaginary axis leave the question to higher-order terms (Hartman--Grobman applies only to hyperbolic equilibria).

\section{Planar systems}
For $2\times2$ real $A$ with trace $\tau$ and determinant $\Delta$, the eigenvalues are $\lambda=\bigl(\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}\bigr)/2$. The equilibrium is a saddle if $\Delta<0$; a node if $\Delta>0$ and $\tau^2\ge4\Delta$; a focus (spiral) if $\Delta>0$ and $\tau^2<4\Delta$; stable iff $\tau<0$ and $\Delta>0$; and a center if $\tau=0<\Delta$ (rotation without decay). This trace--determinant classification is the basis for reading two-dimensional neuron models such as FitzHugh--Nagumo, where a change in input current moves the equilibrium from a stable focus to an unstable one surrounded by a limit cycle (a Hopf bifurcation), producing periodic firing.

\section{The Kalman filter}
For the linear Gaussian model $z_{t+1}=Az_t+w_t$, $y_t=Cz_t+v_t$ with $w_t\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)$, $v_t\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$, the posterior $p(z_t\mid y_{1:t})$ is Gaussian with mean $\hat z_{t\mid t}$ and covariance $P_{t\mid t}$, computed recursively:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\hat z_{t\mid t-1}&=A\hat z_{t-1\mid t-1}, & P_{t\mid t-1}&=AP_{t-1\mid t-1}A^\top+Q,\\
K_t&=P_{t\mid t-1}C^\top(CP_{t\mid t-1}C^\top+R)^{-1}, &&\\
\hat z_{t\mid t}&=\hat z_{t\mid t-1}+K_t(y_t-C\hat z_{t\mid t-1}), & P_{t\mid t}&=(I-K_tC)P_{t\mid t-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The update is the Gaussian conditioning formula applied to the joint distribution of $z_t$ and $y_t$ given the past. The innovation $y_t-C\hat z_{t\mid t-1}$ is white under a correct model, which provides a diagnostic. In brain--computer interfaces, the Kalman filter decodes kinematics $z$ from firing rates $y$; its gain $K_t$ balances the prior dynamics against the new neural evidence.

\section{Observability and identifiability}
The state is \emph{observable} if the initial state can be determined from noiseless outputs. For the linear system this holds iff the observability matrix $[C^\top,(CA)^\top,\ldots,(CA^{n-1})^\top]^\top$ has rank $n$. Observability is about states for known $(A,C)$. \emph{Identifiability} of $(A,C)$ from data is weaker: the substitution $z\mapsto Mz$ for invertible $M$ replaces $(A,C,Q)$ by $(MAM^{-1},CM^{-1},MQM^\top)$ with identical output distributions. Eigenvalues of $A$ (time constants and rotation frequencies) are invariant under this change and are therefore estimable; the individual latent axes are not.

\begin{workedexample}[Rotations are invariant; axes are not]
Fit the rotation model of the previous example with an arbitrary invertible $M$: $A'=MAM^{-1}$. If $M$ is not orthogonal, the trajectory in the new coordinates is an ellipse rather than a circle, but $A'$ still has eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$, and the rotation frequency is unchanged. Statements about ``the rotation frequency'' are identifiable; statements about ``the second latent dimension encodes velocity'' require additional constraints, such as aligning axes to behavioral variables, before they are meaningful.
\end{workedexample}
