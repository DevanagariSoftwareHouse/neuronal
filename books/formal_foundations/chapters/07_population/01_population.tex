\chapter{Neuronal covariance and the geometry of population responses}\label{ch:population}
\begin{readergoal}Distinguish signal correlation, noise correlation, factor structure, and the information represented in population covariance.\end{readergoal}
\section{One neuron versus a population}
Let $r(t)=(r_1(t),\ldots,r_N(t))^\top$ represent firing-rate estimates for $N$ units. A time series $r(t)$ traces a curve through $\mathbb R^N$. Neurons' responses may change together because of shared inputs, latent states, task drive, or interactions. The covariance matrix captures second-order cofluctuation but does not identify which explanation generated it.

In repeated-stimulus experiments, \emph{signal correlation} measures similarity in mean tuning across stimuli; \emph{noise correlation} measures trial-to-trial cofluctuation after controlling the stimulus or condition. These are distinct statistics, and their estimates depend on temporal window, firing rate, spike sorting, and brain state \cite{CohenKohn2011}.

\section{Shared gain and overdispersion}
A modulated Poisson count model can be written $N\mid G\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(G\mu)$, where $G>0$ is a random gain with $E[G]=1$. Then $E[N]=\mu$ and
\[\Var(N)=\mu+\mu^2\Var(G).\]
The second term represents fluctuations in the latent gain; the decomposition follows directly from the law of total variance. For two neurons with gains $G_1,G_2$, shared gain fluctuations can generate positive count covariance. This does not require a direct synaptic link between their spike generators \cite{Goris2014}.

\section{Latent factors and factor analysis}
A linear observation model $r_t=Cz_t+d+\varepsilon_t$ represents high-dimensional responses through lower-dimensional latent $z_t$. Factor analysis separates low-rank shared covariance from modeled observation noise. GPFA adds smooth temporal covariance priors to latent trajectories. Identifying the effective latent dimensionality requires validation and attention to scaling, stationarity, and neuron subsampling \cite{CunninghamYu2014,Gao2017}.

\section{Random-matrix caution}
For a high-dimensional sample covariance, random eigenvalue fluctuations can be large even when the true covariance is isotropic. In a simple independent-Gaussian null with feature-to-sample ratio $q$, the Mar\v{c}enko--Pastur bulk has support $\sigma^2(1\pm\sqrt q)^2$ under the standard asymptotic assumptions. This is not a universal biological noise threshold; correlated trials, heterogeneous variances, and temporal smoothing violate simple null assumptions.

\section{Covariance from a latent factor model}
Under the factor model $r=Cz+d+\varepsilon$ with $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_k)$ independent of $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\Psi)$, $\Psi$ diagonal, the covariance of $r$ is
\[
\Sigma=CC^\top+\Psi.
\]
The \emph{shared} covariance $CC^\top$ has rank $k$; the \emph{private} variance $\Psi$ is neuron-specific. PCA treats all variance alike and therefore mixes the two: a neuron with large private variance attracts a principal component of its own. Factor analysis estimates $C$ and $\Psi$ by maximum likelihood (usually with the EM algorithm). The factor loadings are identifiable only up to rotation, since $C\mapsto CQ$ for orthogonal $Q$ leaves $\Sigma$ unchanged; the \emph{shared subspace} $\operatorname{col}(C)$ is identifiable, the axes within it are not.

\section{Noise correlations and coding}
Consider a population encoding a scalar stimulus $s$ through mean responses $\mu(s)$ with stimulus-independent covariance $\Sigma$. The \emph{linear Fisher information} is
\[
I_{\rm lin}(s)=\mu'(s)^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu'(s).
\]
It bounds the precision of any locally unbiased linear decoder: $\Var(\hat s)\ge1/I_{\rm lin}$. Whether noise correlations help or hurt depends on their \emph{direction} relative to the signal direction $\mu'(s)$, not on their average size.

\begin{proposition}
If $\Sigma=\Sigma_0+\epsilon\,\mu'\mu'^\top$ with $\Sigma_0$ positive definite and $\epsilon>0$, then
\[
I_{\rm lin}=\frac{I_0}{1+\epsilon I_0},\qquad I_0=\mu'^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}\mu',
\]
which is bounded by $1/\epsilon$ no matter how many neurons are recorded.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the Sherman--Morrison formula, $\Sigma^{-1}=\Sigma_0^{-1}-\epsilon\Sigma_0^{-1}\mu'\mu'^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}/(1+\epsilon I_0)$. Hence $\mu'^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mu'=I_0-\epsilon I_0^2/(1+\epsilon I_0)=I_0/(1+\epsilon I_0)$.
\end{proof}
These \emph{differential} or \emph{information-limiting} correlations are small in any single pair but cap the population's information. They are indistinguishable from upstream noise in the stimulus itself, which no downstream circuit can remove.

\section{Estimating correlation from spike counts}
The Pearson correlation of spike counts depends on the counting window and on firing rate even when the underlying dependence is fixed. For two neurons driven by a common Gaussian input and spiking by thresholding, the count correlation grows with window length and with firing rate. Comparing $r_{sc}$ between conditions with different rates therefore requires matching rates or modeling the dependence. Short windows produce correlations close to zero for almost any mechanism, because counts are mostly $0$ or $1$.

\section{Signal versus noise correlation from data}
Given trials $r_{j}^{(s)}$ for stimulus $s$, the noise covariance is estimated within each stimulus and pooled; the signal covariance is the covariance of the stimulus-conditioned means across stimuli. Shuffling trial order independently for each neuron within a stimulus destroys noise correlations while preserving signal correlations, giving a direct test of whether noise correlations affect decoding. With few trials, the estimated means contain noise, which inflates the apparent signal covariance by approximately $\Sigma_{\rm noise}/n_{\rm trials}$; this bias should be removed or cross-validated.

\begin{workedexample}[Information saturation]
Let $N$ neurons have $\mu'_i=1$, private variances $1$, and differential correlations of strength $\epsilon=0.01$. Then $I_0=N$ and $I_{\rm lin}=N/(1+0.01N)$. For $N=10$, $I_{\rm lin}\approx9.1$; for $N=100$, $50$; for $N=1000$, $\approx91$; the limit is $100$. The pairwise correlation coefficient implied is about $0.01/1.01\approx0.01$, below what most experiments can resolve reliably, yet it halves the information of a 100-neuron population.
\end{workedexample}
