\chapter{Counting processes, spike trains, and information}
A spike train is a set of event times or, more generally, a random
locally finite counting measure. Write $N((a,b])$ for the number of
events in an interval. A conditional intensity $\lambda(t\mid
\mathcal H_t)$ describes the conditional instantaneous event rate
given the preceding history $\mathcal H_t$, if such a predictable
intensity exists.

\entry{71}{Kolmogorov extension theorem}
{A consistent collection of finite-dimensional probability distributions
defines a stochastic process on a suitable product measurable space.}
{Build process models from their joint laws across finite time sets.}
\entry{72}{Campbell's theorem}
{For a point process with mean measure $\Lambda$ and nonnegative
measurable $h$, $\E[\sum_{t_i} h(t_i)]=\int h\,d\Lambda$;
signed integrable variants require absolute integrability.}
{Compute expected spike-triggered sums and event statistics.}
\entry{73}{Poisson superposition and thinning}
{Independent Poisson processes superpose to a Poisson process with
the sum intensity; independent thinning with valid retention
probability yields the corresponding Poisson intensity.}
{Generate spike-train simulation benchmarks and control models.}
\entry{74}{Doob--Meyer decomposition}
{A suitable integrable counting submartingale admits a
predictable compensator $A(t)$, and $N(t)-A(t)$ is a martingale;
when absolutely continuous, $A(t)=\int_0^t\lambda(s)\,ds$.}
{Connect conditional intensity estimation and martingale residuals.}
\entry{75}{Time-rescaling theorem}
{Under a correctly specified continuous conditional intensity and
regularity of event times, transformed inter-event compensator
increments are iid $\operatorname{Exp}(1)$ (with standard caveats
for boundaries and conditioning).}
{Construct goodness-of-fit diagnostics for spike-train GLMs.}
\entry{76}{Conditional point-process likelihood}
{For a simple point process with conditional intensity $\lambda$
on a finite observation interval, under standard domination
assumptions its log-likelihood, up to reference terms, is
$\sum_i\log\lambda(t_i)-\int\lambda(t)\,dt$.}
{Derive maximum-likelihood fitting for spiking models.}
\entry{77}{Palm theory and Slivnyak's theorem}
{For homogeneous Poisson processes, a reduced Palm process at a
specified event has the law of the original process; more
general Palm laws need not have this property.}
{Understand event-centered descriptions and null spike correlations.}
\entry{78}{Gibbs' inequality}
{For probability measures $P,Q$ with $P\ll Q$,
$\KL(P\|Q)\geq0$, with equality if $P=Q$ almost everywhere.}
{Understand KL-based divergence estimators and likelihood objectives.}
\entry{79}{Data-processing inequality}
{For Markov chain $X\to Y\to Z$,
$I(X;Z)\leq I(X;Y)$ whenever mutual information is defined.}
{Characterize information lost through feature extraction or decoding.}
\entry{80}{Fano's inequality}
{For a discrete class variable with $K\geq2$ classes,
entropy and classification error obey
$H(X\mid\widehat X)\leq h_2(P_e)+P_e\log(K-1)$,
with a consistent logarithm base.}
{Relate decoder error to information; do not confuse with the
spike-count Fano \emph{factor}.}

\begin{example}[Count likelihood and binning]
With constant intensity $\lambda$ on interval length $\Delta$,
$N_\Delta\sim\Poisson(\lambda\Delta)$.
Then
$\log p(n\mid\lambda)=n\log(\lambda\Delta)
-\lambda\Delta-\log(n!)$.
A binned GLM approximates continuous-time spiking only relative
to its chosen bin width and assumptions about multiple spikes per bin.
\end{example}
\begin{caution}
A time-rescaling test diagnoses discrepancies from the hypothesized
conditional intensity; a passed test is not proof of a unique
physiological mechanism. Model misspecification can also arise from
latent common drive or refractory dynamics.
\end{caution}

\section{Proofs of selected point-process results}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Result 72 for simple functions and Poisson processes]
Let $N$ be a Poisson process with mean measure $\Lambda$ and
$h=\sum_jc_j\ind_{B_j}$ with disjoint bounded sets $B_j$. Then
$\sum_ih(t_i)=\sum_jc_jN(B_j)$ and
$\E\sum_ih(t_i)=\sum_jc_j\Lambda(B_j)=\int h\,d\Lambda$. A nonnegative
measurable $h$ is an increasing limit of such simple functions, and
monotone convergence (Result 22) extends the identity. The same proof
works for any point process with mean measure $\Lambda$, since only
$\E N(B)=\Lambda(B)$ was used.
\end{proof}
For Poisson processes the \emph{variance} is equally explicit:
$\Var\sum_ih(t_i)=\int h^2\,d\Lambda$. Applied to a spike-triggered
sum $y(t)=\sum_ik(t-t_i)$ (shot noise), Campbell's theorem gives
$\E y=\lambda\int k$ and $\Var y=\lambda\int k^2$ for rate $\lambda$:
the mean grows with rate, and the coefficient of variation falls as
$\lambda^{-1/2}$. This is Campbell's original application to
vacuum-tube noise, and the basis of shot-noise LFP models.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Result 73 (superposition)]
If $N_1,N_2$ are independent Poisson processes with intensities
$\lambda_1,\lambda_2$, then for disjoint sets the counts of
$N=N_1+N_2$ are sums of independent Poisson variables, hence Poisson
with means $\Lambda_1(B)+\Lambda_2(B)$, and independent across disjoint
sets because each summand is. These are the defining properties of a
Poisson process with intensity $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$.
\end{proof}
Superposition explains why multi-unit activity summed over many weakly
dependent neurons is often closer to Poisson than any single unit; the
Palm--Khintchine theorem makes this precise for superpositions of many
sparse independent renewal processes.

\section{Deriving the point-process likelihood}
Partition $[0,T]$ into bins of width $\Delta$ and approximate the
conditional probability of a spike in bin $k$ by
$\lambda_k\Delta$, where $\lambda_k=\lambda(t_k\mid\mathcal H_{t_k})$.
By the chain rule of conditional probability, the probability of the
observed binary sequence $(y_k)$ is
\[
\prod_k(\lambda_k\Delta)^{y_k}(1-\lambda_k\Delta)^{1-y_k}.
\]
Taking logarithms and using $\log(1-\lambda_k\Delta)=-\lambda_k\Delta+O(\Delta^2)$,
\[
\sum_ky_k\log\lambda_k+n\log\Delta-\sum_k\lambda_k\Delta+O(\Delta).
\]
The term $n\log\Delta$ does not depend on the model. As $\Delta\to0$,
$\sum_ky_k\log\lambda_k\to\sum_i\log\lambda(t_i)$ and
$\sum_k\lambda_k\Delta\to\int_0^T\lambda(t)\,dt$, which is Result 76.
The derivation exposes the approximation made by a binned GLM: it is
accurate when $\lambda_k\Delta\ll1$ and when at most one spike falls in
a bin. At 1\,ms bins and 100\,spikes/s, $\lambda\Delta=0.1$ and the
neglected terms are no longer negligible.

\section{Information inequalities}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Result 78 (Gibbs), discrete case]
With $p_i>0$ only where $q_i>0$, Jensen's inequality for the concave
logarithm gives
$-\KL(p\|q)=\sum_ip_i\log(q_i/p_i)\le\log\sum_ip_i(q_i/p_i)\le\log1=0$.
Equality in Jensen requires $q_i/p_i$ constant on the support of $p$,
which together with normalization gives $p=q$.
\end{proof}
\begin{proof}[Proof of Result 79 (data processing)]
By the chain rule of mutual information,
$I(X;Y,Z)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y\mid Z)=I(X;Y)+I(X;Z\mid Y)$. For a Markov chain
$X\to Y\to Z$, $X$ and $Z$ are conditionally independent given $Y$, so
$I(X;Z\mid Y)=0$. Hence $I(X;Z)=I(X;Y)-I(X;Y\mid Z)\le I(X;Y)$.
\end{proof}
Every stage of an analysis pipeline (sorting, binning, smoothing,
dimensionality reduction) is a processing step $Y\to Z$ that cannot
increase the information about the stimulus $X$. A decoder applied to
binned counts cannot outperform an optimal decoder applied to the raw
spike times. Information ``gained'' by a feature is information that a
weaker decoder could not otherwise use, not information created.

\begin{example}[Using Fano's inequality]
A decoder classifies 8 movement directions from population activity
with error rate $P_e=0.25$. Result 80 gives
$H(X\mid\hat X)\le h_2(0.25)+0.25\log_2 7\approx0.811+0.702=1.51$\,bits.
With uniform directions $H(X)=3$\,bits, so
$I(X;\hat X)\ge3-1.51=1.49$\,bits, and by data processing the population
activity carries at least that much information about direction.
Decoding accuracy thus gives a \emph{lower} bound on information, not
an estimate of it.
\end{example}

\section*{Exercises for this chapter}
\begin{exercise}
Prove the Poisson case of Campbell's variance formula
$\Var\sum_ih(t_i)=\int h^2\,d\Lambda$ for simple functions.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Compute the mean and variance of shot noise with exponential kernel
$k(t)=Ae^{-t/\tau}\ind_{t\ge0}$ driven by a rate-$\lambda$ Poisson process.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Show that the binned Bernoulli log-likelihood and the binned Poisson
log-likelihood differ by $O(\sum_k\lambda_k^2\Delta^2)$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Prove that $I(X;Y)\ge0$ from Gibbs' inequality.
\end{exercise}
