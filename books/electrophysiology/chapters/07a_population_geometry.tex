\chapter[Population geometry]{Population geometry, detectability, and identifiability}
\label{ch:population-identifiability}
A low-dimensional fit is not necessarily a detectable or
uniquely identified neural mechanism. This chapter separates
three questions: \emph{Is a component present in the data?}
\emph{Can the estimator recover its direction?}
\emph{Does that direction have a unique biological meaning?}

\section{Beyond the Marchenko--Pastur noise edge}
Consider an idealized population covariance
$\Sigma=I+\theta vv^\top$, with $\|v\|=1$,
$\theta>0$, iid Gaussian observations, and
$N/T\to\gamma\in(0,\infty)$.
In the standard spiked-Wishart model, the top population
eigenvalue is $1+\theta$ and the sample-noise edge is
$(1+\sqrt\gamma)^2$.
\begin{theorem}[BBP transition, rank-one covariance case]
When $\theta\le\sqrt\gamma$, the leading sample eigenvalue
approaches the upper noise edge and the sample top direction
does not have asymptotically nonzero alignment with $v$.
When $\theta>\sqrt\gamma$, the sample leading eigenvalue
converges to $(1+\theta)(1+\gamma/\theta)$, strictly above
the edge, and asymptotic alignment is nonzero.
\end{theorem}
The result belongs to the spiked-covariance phase-transition family
\cite{baik2005}. It depends on the iid isotropic sampling model.
Temporal dependence, heterogeneous neuron variances, preprocessing,
and correlated noise modify its calibration.
A detectable statistical spike is not automatically an
identified cell assembly or an anatomical network.

\section{Shrinkage is part of covariance estimation}
For sample covariance $S$ of $N$ variables and a target
$T_0$ (often proportional to the identity), a linear shrinkage
estimator is $\widehat\Sigma=(1-\alpha)S+\alpha T_0$.
The optimal $\alpha$ depends on the loss, assumptions,
and estimation method. Ledoit--Wolf-type procedures estimate
shrinkage to reduce high-dimensional covariance risk
\cite{ledoitwolf2004};
James--Stein concerns shrinkage of normal means in its classical
form. Neither is a license to tune hyperparameters on held-out
test trials.

\section{Nonuniqueness of latent coordinates}
In Gaussian factor analysis, $y=\Lambda z+\epsilon$ with
$z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, the rotation
$\Lambda'=\Lambda Q$, $z'=Q^\top z$ for orthogonal $Q$
leaves the observable covariance $\Lambda\Lambda^\top+\Psi$
unchanged. The latent axes are therefore not uniquely labeled.
For a linear state-space model,
\[
x_{t+1}=Ax_t+Bu_t+w_t,\qquad y_t=Cx_t+v_t,
\]
any invertible $T$ changes coordinates to
$A'=TAT^{-1}$, $B'=TB$, $C'=CT^{-1}$,
with transformed process-noise covariance, while preserving
the measured trajectories. This is \emph{similarity ambiguity},
not an estimator bug. General nonlinear latent-variable models
may have even larger coordinate freedom.

\section{Alignment and comparison of subspaces}
For matrices $X,Y\in\mathbb R^{n\times k}$ of aligned,
centered samples, the orthogonal Procrustes problem is
\[
\min_{Q^\top Q=I}\|XQ-Y\|_F^2.
\]
\begin{proposition}[Procrustes solution]
Let $X^\top Y=U\Sigma V^\top$ be an SVD. Then
$Q_*=UV^\top$ is an optimal orthogonal alignment.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Expanding the squared norm leaves the variable term
$-2\operatorname{tr}(Q^\top X^\top Y)$.
Writing $Q=U Z V^\top$, the trace is
$\operatorname{tr}(Z^\top\Sigma)\le\sum_i\sigma_i$,
maximized by $Z=I$.
\end{proof}
Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) instead finds directions
with maximal \emph{cross-view correlation} after whitening both
covariance structures. Representational similarity analysis (RSA)
and centered kernel alignment (CKA) compare geometries through
different invariances. They require carefully matched observations,
preprocessing and independent validation sets.

\begin{example}[Existing code]
\path{population_pca.m} produces an SVD of centered
recordings. \path{participation_ratio.m} summarizes
a covariance eigenspectrum. Neither establishes BBP significance,
latent-factor uniqueness, or a well-identified inter-area
communication subspace without additional modeling and tests.
\end{example}

\section{Canonical correlation analysis as an SVD}
Let $X\in\R^{T\times p}$ and $Y\in\R^{T\times q}$ be centered, with
covariance blocks $\Sigma_{XX},\Sigma_{YY}\succ0$ and $\Sigma_{XY}$.
CCA seeks $a,b$ maximizing $\operatorname{corr}(Xa,Yb)$.
\begin{proposition}
The canonical correlations are the singular values of the whitened
cross-covariance $K=\Sigma_{XX}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_{YY}^{-1/2}$, and the
canonical directions are $a=\Sigma_{XX}^{-1/2}u$, $b=\Sigma_{YY}^{-1/2}v$ for
the corresponding singular vector pairs $(u,v)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Substitute $a=\Sigma_{XX}^{-1/2}u$ and $b=\Sigma_{YY}^{-1/2}v$. The
correlation becomes $u^\top Kv/(\|u\|\|v\|)$, which by the variational
characterization of singular values is maximized by the top singular
pair with value $\sigma_1$. Subsequent pairs maximize subject to
orthogonality, giving the remaining singular values.
\end{proof}
When $p$ or $q$ approaches $T$, the sample covariances are
ill-conditioned and the leading sample canonical correlations approach
one even for independent data. Regularized CCA replaces
$\Sigma_{XX}$ by $\Sigma_{XX}+\kappa I$, and canonical correlations must be
assessed on held-out data. Two brain areas with ``canonical correlation
0.9'' in-sample may share almost nothing out of sample.

\section{Reduced-rank regression}
For $Y\approx XB$ with $\rank B\le r$, minimizing
$\|Y-XB\|_F^2$ has a closed form. Let $\hat B_{\rm OLS}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y$
and let $V_r$ hold the top $r$ right singular vectors of the fitted values
$X\hat B_{\rm OLS}$. Then $\hat B_r=\hat B_{\rm OLS}V_rV_r^\top$.
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\|Y-XB\|_F^2=\|Y-X\hat B_{\rm OLS}\|_F^2+\|X\hat B_{\rm OLS}-XB\|_F^2$ by
orthogonality of the OLS residual to $\operatorname{col}(X)$. The second
term is minimized over rank-$r$ matrices $XB$ by Eckart--Young
(Result 29) applied to $X\hat B_{\rm OLS}$; the minimizer is its
projection onto $V_r$, which is $X\hat B_{\rm OLS}V_rV_r^\top$.
\end{proof}
The row space of $\hat B_r$ (spanned by $V_r$) is the \emph{predictive
subspace} of the target area. Its dimension, chosen by cross-validation,
is the ``communication subspace'' dimension in inter-areal studies. It
measures how many linear combinations of the target are predictable from
the source; it does not say that the source causes those combinations.

\section{Centered kernel alignment and RSA}
For representations $X\in\R^{n\times p}$, $Y\in\R^{n\times q}$ of the same
$n$ stimuli (columns centered), linear CKA is
\[
\mathrm{CKA}(X,Y)=\frac{\|Y^\top X\|_F^2}{\|X^\top X\|_F\,\|Y^\top Y\|_F}.
\]
It is invariant to orthogonal transformations and isotropic scaling of
either representation, but not to arbitrary invertible linear maps
(unlike CCA, which is invariant to them). This intermediate invariance
is often what one wants when comparing neural populations whose
individual neurons have no correspondence but whose relative variance
across directions is meaningful. RSA compares the $n\times n$
dissimilarity matrices of the two representations with a rank
correlation; it is invariant to any transformation preserving the chosen
dissimilarity ordering. Both methods require the same stimuli in the
same order, and both are biased by noise in the dissimilarity
estimates: cross-validated (split-half) distances remove the positive
bias that noise adds to every Euclidean distance.

\begin{example}[A spiked model below and above threshold]
With $N=200$ neurons, $T=400$ samples ($\gamma=0.5$) and a planted
rank-one spike of strength $\theta$, the BBP threshold is
$\sqrt{0.5}\approx0.71$. A spike with $\theta=0.5$ (variance $1.5$ in its
direction, versus $1$ elsewhere) is undetectable by the top eigenvalue:
that eigenvalue sits near the noise edge $(1+\sqrt{0.5})^2\approx2.91$.
With $\theta=2$, the top eigenvalue converges to
$(1+2)(1+0.25)=3.75$, clearly above the edge, and the squared alignment
of the sample eigenvector with the planted direction is
$(1-\gamma/\theta^2)/(1+\gamma/\theta)=0.875/1.25=0.7$. Detectability and
direction recovery both depend on $\theta$ \emph{relative to} $\sqrt\gamma$,
so doubling the number of samples can turn an invisible component into a
reliable one.
\end{example}

\section*{Exercises for this chapter}
\begin{exercise}
Show that canonical correlations are invariant to invertible linear
transformations of $X$ and of $Y$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Show that linear CKA is invariant to $X\mapsto cXQ$ for orthogonal $Q$
and $c\ne0$, and give a linear map that changes it.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
In the spiked example, find the smallest $T$ for which a spike of
strength $\theta=0.5$ becomes detectable with $N=200$.
\end{exercise}
\begin{exercise}
Derive the Procrustes solution when reflections are not allowed
($\det Q=1$).
\end{exercise}
